Maya a1: Do the Options Read the Model, or Does Their Position? Order Sensitivity in a Non-Autoregressive Decision Model

Quantifying option-order dependence in Laya and removing most of it by shuffled fine-tuning

Mohammed Nayeem Khan

Logesh L 

mohammed.nayeem.khan@intel.comlogesh.l@intel.com

Intel Corporation

Abstract

Laya answers typed multiple-choice questions in one forward pass by scoring a hidden state at one [MASK] marker per option. We re-engineer the checkpoint (421M parameters; a 2-layer option head with no positional tensor; a documented but unused parallel layout) and test whether the answer depends on where an option is listed. Presenting 951 Qwen-verified questions under every cyclic rotation of their options (or six random permutations for 12 options), the released checkpoint changes its answer for 25.8% [22.9–28.7] of questions (19.7% on the 605 English-language items alone); accuracy with the gold option first versus last differs by 10.1%, and the flips concentrate where the model’s own confidence is low (54% versus 8% across confidence terciles). Fine-tuning with Laya’s trainer on 2,818 teacher-verified rows with option shuffling cuts the flip rate to 7.2%; the identical rows without shuffling give 11.4%, so shuffling is responsible for 4.2% of the improvement. A confidence-weighted vote over orderings gives 69.7% against 69.1% for a single base pass at 4.1× the compute. Labels are LLM-made and the test sets are teacher-filtered. We also fine-tune with the authors’ parallel layout on the same rows: flip rate 0.5%.

Keywords: non-autoregressive decision models; Laya; re-engineering; calibration; LLM-generated supervision; fine-tuning.

1 Introduction

A decision model that reads option texts should give the same answer when the same options are listed in a different order. Laya is a non-autoregressive encoder that answers typed questions in one forward pass; for a choice question it appends one [MASK] marker per option to the question and scores the hidden state at each marker. The options are therefore laid out along a single sequence, and nothing in the weights guarantees that an option’s position is irrelevant.

We measure this on 951 Qwen-verified choice questions drawn from seven categories (3, 5 and 12 options), presenting each question under every cyclic rotation of its options (K≤5) or six random permutations (K=12), i.e. 3,893 model calls per checkpoint.

Research questions. RQ1: does the released checkpoint change its answer under reordering, and by how much? RQ2: what is the shape of the dependence (slot, option count, category, confidence)? RQ3: is a no-training remedy (voting over orderings) competitive? RQ4: does fine-tuning on Qwen-verified rows with option shuffling remove the dependence, and how much of the effect is due to shuffling itself rather than the extra data? RQ5: is the result stable across training seeds?

Contributions. (i) On the base checkpoint 25.8% [22.9–28.7] of questions change their answer purely because the options were reordered (19.7% on the 605 English-language items alone). (ii) The dependence is not a first/last artefact alone: the first-vs-last accuracy gap is 10.1%, but predictions are concentrated in particular slots (total-variation distance from uniform 0.09 at K=3, 0.07 at K=12). (iii) Fine-tuning with shuffling reduces the flip rate to 7.2%, whereas the same rows without shuffling give 11.4%. (iv) Confidence is an informative detector of flips (flip rate 54% / 16% / 8% across confidence terciles) and a confidence-weighted vote over orderings gives 69.7% versus 69.1% for a single base pass, at 4.1× compute. (v) We also fine-tune with the authors’ parallel layout on the same rows: flip rate 0.5%.

2 Background and related work

Position bias in generative models. Multiple-choice evaluation of decoder LLMs is known to depend on where the answer is listed: models prefer particular option IDs or positions, and accuracy moves by several points when options are permuted [Zheng et al. 2023; Pezeshkpour and Hruschka 2023]; LLM judges prefer the first response shown [Wang et al. 2023]. Surface-form competition [Holtzman et al. 2021] adds that probability mass is spread over strings, not options. These analyses concern models that generate an option label; Laya does not generate, it reads one hidden state per option, so the mechanism, and the available remedies, differ.

Order-invariant architectures. Set-style encoders avoid order by construction. Laya’s authors identified the issue in their own code: a docstring states that in the sequential layout “the slot logits of five identical options spread by 3.59” and the repository contains an unused parallel layout that shares position ids across options and masks cross-option attention. This paper does not replace that effort; it asks how large the effect is on realistic questions, what it looks like, and whether data alone (option shuffling during fine-tuning) removes most of it.

Positional and surface-form effects beyond LLMs. Option-order effects are not confined to decoder LLMs. In multiple-choice reading comprehension with fine-tuned encoders, answer position is a frequent artefact of dataset construction; here the dataset construction is uniform by design (every option is gold equally often in every slot), so any effect we measure belongs to the model. In LLM evaluation, the standard mitigation is to average over permutations at inference [Zheng et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023]; we test that (Section 5.6) alongside a training-time mitigation and an architectural one.

Why a separate study for a non-generative model. The mitigations proposed for generative models, such as debiasing the prior over option IDs, assume a probability distribution over answer tokens. Laya has no such distribution: it has one scalar logit per slot. The relevant questions are therefore different: is the logit a function of the option text alone, or of its position? Is the dependence learned from the training order or built into the positional machinery of the encoder? The identical-option probe (Section 6) and the layout comparison (Section 7) answer these two questions separately.

Evaluation protocol. We follow the permutation-invariance literature in using all cyclic rotations for small K (so that each option occupies each slot exactly once) and random permutations for large K, and we report the flip rate (an itemlevel instability measure) next to mean accuracy, because accuracy averaged over orderings can hide that individual decisions change.

Why a non-generative model could still be position-biased. Encoders with position information (absolute or rotary) can learn position-dependent shortcuts whenever label frequency correlates with position during training. In Laya’s own

training recipe the options are presented in the order the question lists them unless the opt-in --shuffle-options flag is used (Laya’s trainer docstring: “The scripts always present options in the order the question lists them, so the model can learn a position prior”). Our data-generation process makes gold labels uniform over slots by construction, so any position prior we measure is a property of the released weights, not of our data.

3.3 Runtime path

We read the open-source runtime ( laya 0.4.26) alongside the weights; the path from request to probability is:

  1. Agent.predict_batch ( agent.py ~L1486) groups states that share a question set and tokenizes each state once.

  2. build_head / build_sequence ( common.py L157–L270) render [CLS] <type> question: <instructions> [SEP] [MASK] opt0 … [MASK] optK−1 [SEP] <state> [SEP] ; each option text is capped at 48 tokens, the head at head_max_len , and the state is cut from the right to fit max_len ( truncate_left=False ).

  3. The encoder runs once. The hidden state at each option’s [MASK] marker is taken as that option’s slot state; the type embedding is added and the 2-layer head lets the slots attend to each other.

  4. The scorer emits one logit per slot. For choice the logits are divided by the bucket temperature ( temp_bucket(type, K) , clamped to [0.5, 5]) and soft-maxed; score uses the same softmax and reports an expected level; noul reports the probability of the second slot.

  5. unpermute_probs maps probabilities back if the caller supplied option_order ; the result carries answer_confidence (the top probability) and a separate entropy-based confidence that is explicitly not calibrated.

Figure RE-1. The decision path of Laya as reconstructed from the tensor inventory and the open-source runtime. Red: the two places where the studies in this series find problems (positional dependence through the encoder; the option-count temperature).

Note on the unused parallel layout. uses_parallel_layout(cfg) reads option_layout from the config and defaults to sequential ; the base config has no such key. The docstring in common.py says a checkpoint trained on one layout reads the other “as a different input”, so switching layouts requires training, which is outside this paper.

3.4 Hypotheses from the re-engineering

H1 (position enters through the encoder). The head has no positional-embedding tensor (Table RE-1 lists none): its two layers are permutation-equivariant over slots, and the type embedding is the same for every slot. The only way an option’s position can influence its logit is through the encoder, whose rotary positions put each [MASK] marker at a different offset in the sequential layout. Prediction: answers change under reordering although the option texts are identical.

H2 (the effect is largest for the first and last slot and for many options). Rotary attention makes the first marker adjacent to the instruction and the last adjacent to the state; both are privileged. Prediction: a U-shaped accuracy curve over the gold slot, strongest at K=12.

H3 (shuffled fine-tuning is sufficient). If the dependence is learned rather than architectural, presenting options in random order during fine-tuning should remove it without touching the layout. Prediction: v1 flips far less than the noshuffle ablation trained on identical rows.

4.1 Held-out data from an LLM teacher

We cannot ship human-labelled test sets for these gaps, so we generate them. For each category a prompt asks Qwen3.8-27B (thinking disabled) for ten realistic inputs whose correct answer is a stated label , with category hints (for example, “each text must contain a negation”). A second, independent call is shown the same inputs without the intended label and asked to choose; any input on which the two calls disagree is discarded. Exact prompts are in Appendix A. 4.3 Statistics

Proportions carry Wilson 95% intervals [Wilson 1927]. Two models on the same items are compared with the exact two-sided McNemar test [McNemar 1947]. Rates that are not per-item correctness (flip rate, ECE) use a row-level bootstrap with 1,000 resamples (300 where a statistic is expensive) [Efron and Tibshirani 1993]. We use α=0.01 and a minimum practical effect of 5 points, fixed before the fix results were computed (see papers.py ); no multiplicity correction is applied across the ten gaps we scanned, which is why we require both a small p-value and a non-trivial effect. ECE uses 10 equal-width bins unless stated.

4.4 Leakage audit

Exact duplicates between test and training rows are removed during generation. To check for near-duplicates we computed, for every test item, the character-5-gram Jaccard similarity to its most similar training row. Median maximum similarity is 0.08–0.15 across sets, and 0.1% of items on average reach 0.5 (worst set: 0.5%). Accuracy on the low-similarity subset equals overall accuracy to within 0.0 points, so we find no sign that gains come from memorised neighbours.

4.5 Order-sensitivity protocol

Questions are taken from every test set whose question type is choice with K≥3 (951 items; categories in Table 2). For K≤5 the options are rotated cyclically, giving K orderings in which every option appears in every slot once; for K=12 six random permutations are drawn once (seed 1) and reused for every item and every model, so all models see identical presentations. Flip rate is the fraction of items whose predicted option differs across orderings. Gold-slot accuracy averages correctness over all (item, ordering) pairs in which the gold option sits in a given slot. Vote accuracy counts an item as correct only if the gold option is the unique plurality answer over orderings. Flip rate has a non-zero floor on ambiguous items, so we always compare models on identical items and use the no-shuffle ablation as the matched control.

5.4 Where the predictions land

Figure 2. Distribution of the slot chosen by the model. Because every option appears in every slot equally often (rotations), an orderblind model would follow the dashed line apart from genuine errors.

Under rotations every option occupies every slot equally often, so the share of predictions per slot should be near 1/K for an order-blind model that is mostly right. The total-variation distance from uniform is 0.092 (K=3) and 0.069 (K=12) for the base model, 0.024 / 0.048 without shuffling and 0.002 / 0.049 with it.

Largely yes. For the base model the flip rate falls from 54% in the least-confident tercile to 16% and 8%; among the 20% of questions on which it is most confident, 2.6% still flip. Confidence is therefore a useful, imperfect detector: a caller that abstains or re-queries with a shuffled order when confidence is low removes most unstable items, but 8% of even the most confident tercile can change answer under reordering. For Maya-v1 the corresponding figures are 74% / 29% / 1%, with most items in the top tercile.

5.6 A no-training alternative: voting over orderings

Because Laya exposes option_order , a caller can average over reorderings at inference time. Taking a confidenceweighted vote over all 4.1 orderings (on average) moves the base model from 69.1% (mean single-pass accuracy) to 69.7% (a gain of +0.6 points) at 4.1× the compute. A single pass of Maya-v1 reaches 86.3%; voting over Maya-v1 reaches 86.2%. Voting does not rescue the base model: its errors are largely consistent across orderings (the item is simply hard or out of its language), so averaging orderings cannot fix them; what shuffling during fine-tuning changes is the model itself.

5.7 How many orderings does an audit need?

Figure 3. How many random orderings must be tried before an unstable item shows two different answers, by option count (base model; items that flip under the full set of orderings). K=3 has only three orderings, K=5 five, K=12 six.

A practical question for anyone who wants to measure order sensitivity on their own schema: trying two random orderings exposes 77% of the unstable items among the 3-option questions (the full set has only three orderings), 56% among 5-option and 48% among 12-option questions; four orderings expose 92% (K=5) and 82% (K=12). A twoordering audit therefore misses a substantial share of unstable items on every option count; the figures above show how that share shrinks as more orderings are tried.

5.8 Unstable items are the wrong ones

Order sensitivity is not a cosmetic problem. The base model’s mean accuracy is 41.6% on the 245 items that flip and 78.6% on those that do not; for Maya-v1 the figures are 43.7% on 68 flipping items and 89.6% on stable ones. Reducing flips therefore removes a pool of low-accuracy decisions rather than only reshuffling equally accurate ones.

The base checkpoint is far from uniform at K=12: its most probable slot receives 18% against 4% for the least probable (uniform: 8.3%), a 4.9× ratio. Fine-tuning with shuffling (Maya-v1) lowers the ratio only to 2.2× and the no-shuffle ablation to 1.4×; the parallel layout brings it to 1.22× (essentially flat). This qualifies the headline result of the previous section: shuffled finetuning removes most content-dependent flips on realistic questions, but a latent position prior remains whenever the content is uninformative, and only the architectural change removes it. The letter prior (orange in Figure X-2) is a separate effect: the model also prefers some option names , and the parallel layout does not remove that.

7 Fix: fine-tuning with option shuffling

Maya-v1 is Laya fine-tuned on 2,818 Qwen-verified rows (Table M-3) with the trainer’s --shuffle-options , which reencodes each choice item every epoch under a random option order and permutes the target accordingly. Its flip rate is 7.2% [5.7–8.9]. The paired reduction relative to the base model is 18.6% [15.6–21.7]; fix criterion (≥5 points, interval excluding 0): met .

7.1 Attribution: shuffling versus extra data

Training on exactly the same rows without shuffling gives a flip rate of 11.4% [9.5–13.6]. The difference attributable to shuffling is 4.2% [2.2–6.4]. The remainder of the improvement over the base (14.4%) is what fine-tuning on these rows alone buys, presumably because it teaches the model to rely on option content for these schemas. Attribution is therefore not all-or-nothing: shuffling matters, and so does task data.

7.3 Cost

Accuracy averaged over orderings moves from 69.1% to 86.3%. The fix therefore does not trade accuracy for stability on these schemas. Training takes about five minutes on one consumer GPU; inference cost is unchanged (one pass).

7.4 The authors’ architectural fix: the parallel layout

The trainer exposes option_layout ; with "parallel" every option starts at the same position id and options may not attend to one another, so reordering only reorders marker embeddings. We fine-tuned the base checkpoint with exactly the Maya-v1 rows and recipe except for this layout ( train_parallel.py ; same seed, shuffling on). Its flip rate is 0.5% [0.1–1.1], versus 7.2% for the sequential Maya-v1 (paired difference sequential − parallel 6.6% [4.9–8.4]) and 25.8% for the base model ([22.4–28.2] reduction). Mean accuracy over orderings is 86.0% versus 86.3%. Its flip rate is essentially zero, as expected if the layout makes the encoder blind to option order; the data-side fix (shuffling) therefore recovers 74% of the architectural fix’s reduction.

13 Discussion and deployment guidance

  • If you deploy the released checkpoint unchanged, assume that about 26% of choice questions are orderdependent; shuffle and vote for high-stakes decisions, or log the order you used.

  • If you can fine-tune, use option shuffling; it costs nothing at inference and is as effective as the 4.1-way vote in our data.

  • If you control training from scratch, the parallel layout is the principled fix; our adaptation of the base checkpoint to it gives flip rate 0.5% (Section 6.4).

  • Use confidence to triage. Table 3 shows that low-confidence questions are where answers flip; abstaining or reasking with a shuffled order on low confidence removes most unstable items for the base model, but a minority of high-confidence answers still flip.

What this paper does not show. We do not claim that every question schema behaves like ours, that shuffled finetuning is better than the parallel layout, or that order invariance on teacher-labelled data implies invariance on humanlabelled data.

14 Threats to validity

  • Ambiguity floor. Items on which two options are both plausible flip under any model. We therefore compare on identical items and report the no-shuffle control, but the absolute flip rate is an upper bound on pure position

effects.

  • Permutation sampling. For K=12 we sample six of 12! orderings, so slot-resolved curves are estimates; the same sample is used for all models.

  • Teacher circularity. Training rows and test rows are written by the same LLM family; a gain can reflect learning the teacher’s style. The leakage audit rules out near-duplicates, not stylistic regularities. A human-labelled replication is the single most important follow-up.

  • Single backbone and question schema family. We study Laya’s English root (ModernBERT-large) and, where stated, its multilingual checkpoint. Other schemas or checkpoints (e.g. the vision lane) may behave differently.

  • Statistical power. Several held-out sets have under 100 items; we report intervals and avoid claims the intervals do not support.

  • Upstream awareness. Some symptoms we measure are acknowledged in Laya’s own source (we cite where). Our contribution is measurement on realistic questions and a tested remedy, not discovery priority.

15 Limitations and future work

What this paper does not establish

  • Mechanism. We show that the dependence is carried by the encoder’s treatment of position (the parallel layout removes it) but we do not open the model: no attention-head or hidden-state analysis is attempted.

  • Schema coverage. Seven question schemas with 3, 5 and 12 options; no schema with long option descriptions, hierarchical options or more than 12 options.

  • Parallel layout. We adapt a sequentially-trained checkpoint for four epochs; training the parallel layout from the pre-trained encoder with a large corpus could behave differently (better accuracy, or different residual priors).

  • Label source. Gold labels are LLM-made; an independent LLM agrees with them on 94–99% of items (see the validity section), but humans were not consulted.

Future work

  1. Train the parallel layout from scratch and compare with shuffled sequential training at equal data.

  2. Test whether the latent prior (identical-option probe) affects deployed accuracy on genuinely ambiguous inputs, where the prior would break ties.

  3. Test order-averaged inference ( option_order ) on top of the parallel layout.

  4. Repeat on human-labelled data and on schemas from real deployments.

16 Alternative explanations

We considered the following alternative explanations of the main result; for each we state what in the data speaks to it.

  • Label noise makes items flip. Possible for ambiguous items, but the no-shuffle ablation uses the same rows and labels and flips on 11.4%, versus 7.2% with shuffling; label noise is held constant across these two.

  • Sharper confidence hides flips. Flip rate is computed on the arg-max option, which is invariant to temperature, so changes in confidence cannot produce or remove flips.

  • The rotation design favours some models. Every option appears in every slot equally often for K≤5, and the same six permutations are used for K=12 for every model.

  • The fine-tuning rows teach an order prior. Gold options in the generated rows are uniformly distributed over labels by construction, and the no-shuffle ablation, which trains on the same rows, keeps a larger flip rate; the rows alone do not remove the dependence.

17 Ethics and broader impact

Intended use. This is measurement of a model’s behaviour and a data-side remedy; it is not a deployment recommendation. Decision models route and triage; wrong answers have different costs in different workflows, and we do not estimate those costs.

Data. All training and test text was generated by an LLM; no personal data, customer messages or proprietary records were used. Generated text may contain stereotypes or errors; we did not audit it for bias beyond the label-consistency filter.

Credentials. The teacher endpoint is accessed with an API key read from the environment; no key appears in the released code, data or this document.

Dual use. The injection and shortcut probes in this series describe how to make a decision model answer wrongly. They are simple and publicly motivated, and we think measurement is more useful to defenders than harmful to them.

18 Conclusion

Laya’s released checkpoints are order-sensitive on realistic questions: 25.8% of questions flip when only the option order changes. Data alone (shuffled fine-tuning on a few thousand teacher-verified rows) reduces this to 7.2%, and the no-shuffle ablation shows that the shuffling, not merely the rows, is responsible for 4.2% of it. The architectural remedy the authors built (parallel layout) gives flip rate 0.5% when the base checkpoint is adapted to it with the same rows (Section 6.4).

Appendix D Metric definitions

Definitions

  • Accuracy. Fraction of items whose highest-probability option equals the gold option; Wilson 95% interval.

  • Confidence. The probability of the predicted option ( answer_confidence ), which is what Laya’s own calibration is fitted on; not the entropy-based confidence field, which is a different quantity.

  • ECE. With B equal-width confidence bins, ECE = Σb (nb/n)|accb − confb|; we use B=10 (B=15 in the pooled

  • calibration study). It is biased upward for small n.

  • McNemar. Exact two-sided binomial test on the discordant pairs n01, n10 of two classifiers on the same items.

  • Bootstrap. Resample items with replacement; percentile 95% interval.

  • MDE. Minimum detectable paired difference at 80% power: (z1−α/2 + z0.80)·√(d/n), where d is the discordance rate.
