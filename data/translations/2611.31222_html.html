CTLink: A Text-Prompted Segmentation Model for Linking Radiology Report Findings to 3D Chest CT Scans

Abstract

Radiology reports describe findings in text only, without visually marking them on the scan, so readers cannot directly see which abnormality each reported finding refers to. We introduce CTLink, a text-prompted 3D segmentation model that segments findings described in a chest CT report. CTLink was trained on 4123 noncontrast chest CT scans with 9547 segmented report findings, including ReXGroundingCTv2, a newly annotated dataset of 1134 scans and 1895 findings with every instance of each finding segmented. On the 200 ReXGroundingCT validation scans, CTLink achieved a hit rate of 0.816, an instance F1 score of 0.456, and a mean Dice score of 0.400, compared with 0.533, 0.175, and 0.234 for VoxTell, a publicly available text-prompted segmentation model (P<.001P<.001 for all). On the test set of the MICCAI 2026 ReXGrounding challenge, an ensemble of seven CTLink models achieved the highest hit rate (0.782) and instance F1 score (0.386) of all 48 participating teams. On 50 test scans segmented twice by independent radiologists, CTLink matched or exceeded inter-rater agreement. Diffuse non-focal findings remained the main limitation, partly because their extent is outlined inconsistently even between radiologists. The CTLink model weights are available at https://huggingface.co/rajpurkarlab/CTLink, and the dataset at https://huggingface.co/datasets/rajpurkarlab/ReXGroundingCT.

Introduction

Radiologists record the findings of an imaging examination in a radiology report, which describes each finding in text only, without marking it on the scan. Referring clinicians who want to see a described finding must therefore locate it on the scan themselves, which takes time and can be ambiguous when a scan contains several similar abnormalities. When findings are visually marked on the scan, referring clinicians plan management faster and more confidently (1). A model that segments the finding described by each report sentence would automatically highlight that finding on the scan, linking every sentence to the abnormality it describes. Such a model would spare referring clinicians the search for each described finding, make it easier to compare findings with those on prior examinations, and make AI-generated reports more verifiable (2, 3, 4).

For three-dimensional scans such as chest CT, the task of segmenting the findings described by report sentences remains difficult (5). A report sentence specifies a finding by its appearance, size, and location, often among several similar abnormalities in the same scan. Most segmentation models, however, are trained to segment a fixed set of organs or lesion types (6, 7), accepting only a category name, such as “nodule” or “tumor,” as the prompt (8, 9, 10, 11). Such models perform poorly on free-text report sentences (5), because these sentences differ from the short category names the models were trained on. Linking phrases to the image regions they describe, a task known as visual grounding (12, 13), has been studied in radiology mainly on chest radiographs, with bounding boxes rather than segmentations (14, 15, 16, 17, 3). More recent work on CT has linked report text to anatomical regions, such as organs or lung lobes (18, 19), or has localized findings with bounding boxes (20), rather than segmenting the described findings. Progress has long been limited by the lack of a public dataset that pairs report sentences with segmentations of the described findings. ReXGroundingCT now provides such data, linking findings from chest CT reports (21) to 3D segmentation masks (4). Models trained with ReXGroundingCT, such as VoxTell (22) and DAGG (23), have substantially advanced performance on this task but have not reached the level of agreement between radiologists (4).

We introduce CTLink, a text-prompted segmentation model for linking radiology report findings to 3D chest CT scans. To train it, we create ReXGroundingCTv2, which adds 1134 chest CT scans with every instance of each finding segmented to ReXGroundingCT. On the ReXGroundingCT validation set, CTLink localizes 82% of findings, compared with 53% for VoxTell. On 50 test scans segmented twice by independent radiologists (4), CTLink matches or exceeds inter-rater agreement. On the test set of the ReXGrounding challenge at MICCAI 2026, an ensemble of CTLink models localizes more findings (78%) and achieves a higher instance F1 score (0.39) than the entries of all 48 participating teams (24). We publicly release the CTLink model weights and ReXGroundingCTv2 to further support research in this direction.

Materials and Methods

Datasets and Annotations

This retrospective study used de-identified noncontrast chest CT scans and radiology reports from CT-RATE (21), a public dataset acquired during routine care at Istanbul Medipol University Mega Hospital, Turkey, and released under its own ethics approval. Additional institutional review board approval was not required. Table 1 summarizes the datasets used for training and evaluation. The training set of the original ReXGroundingCT (v1 training set) contained 2992 scans and 7687 findings, with at most three instances annotated per finding (4). We used the corrected v1 masks released by Team 4 of the ReXGrounding challenge (D.K., J.I.), who made 146 changes to 117 scans (97 masks redrawn, 35 findings removed, and 14 other edits, such as sentence fixes and side corrections), leaving 2989 scans and 7652 findings (24). As part of ReXGroundingCTv2, we annotated 1134 additional training scans with 1895 findings (v2 training set). The 200 ReXGrounding challenge validation scans (381 findings) were used for the ablation, and the 300 ReXGrounding challenge test scans (582 findings) were used for the comparison with the challenge entries. Eight validation scans and 15 test scans came from patients who also had a different examination among the scans used for training.

Finding sentences came from the CT-RATE reports, which had been translated from Turkish into English. For ReXGroundingCT, a large language model rewrote each report into standard terminology and split it into individual findings (4). Only findings of the lungs, airways, and pleura were kept, so findings of other structures, such as the mediastinum or bones, were not annotated. Each finding sentence had one reference mask, drawn on a cloud-based annotation platform with brush, polygon, and region-growing tools, in which each instance of the finding (for example, each nodule) was labeled separately. The v1 training masks included at most three instances of each finding, whereas the v2 masks, just like the validation and test masks, included every visible instance. The annotation process is described in more detail in Appendix Supplemental Material. Findings that could not be localized, were too diffuse to outline, or described normal anatomy were not annotated.

Model

CTLink used the same architecture as VoxTell v1.1 (22): a residual-encoder 3D U-Net (6) image encoder and a transformer prompt decoder. In the prompt decoder, the sentence embedding, computed by a frozen Qwen3-Embedding-4B model (25), attended to the image features, and the resulting text features were fused with the decoder features at every stage. Each finding sentence was one prompt, and the output was a binary mask in the native CT voxel grid. Figure 1 shows the architecture.

Training

All models were fine-tuned from the VoxTell v1.1 weights. This version was trained on 190 public datasets and drew only 5% of its training samples from instance-focused datasets such as ReXGroundingCT (22), so report sentences were a small part of its training. Each training sample was one scan–finding pair, with a random 3D crop of 1923192^{3} voxels that was centered on the finding in 50% of samples. Training used a combined Dice and binary cross-entropy loss with deep supervision and the AdamW optimizer (learning rate 1×10−51\times 10^{-5}, 500-iteration linear warm-up, cosine decay) for 50 000 iterations with a batch size of two (100 000 training samples). For v1 findings with three annotated instances, background voxels were down-weighted (weight, 0.3), because further instances may be unlabeled.

The ablation (Table 2) started from the published VoxTell model without fine-tuning and added one change per row. Oversampling drew the v2 scans three times as often as the v1 scans, larger input crops increased the crop size from 1923192^{3} to 192×256×256192\times 256\times 256 voxels, and longer training increased the number of iterations from 50 000 to 75 000 and the effective batch size from two to four (300 000 instead of 100 000 training samples). No ablation model was trained on validation scans.

For the comparison with the ReXGrounding challenge entries on the 300 challenge test scans, CTLink was retrained with the same recipe on the training scans plus the 200 validation scans (sampled as often as v2 scans), with a batch size of four. The final checkpoint was used, without any selection on test data. Models were trained on NVIDIA L40S (48 GB) or H100 (80 GB) GPUs. The CTLink model for the ReXGrounding challenge comparison was trained on one H100 GPU.

Post-processing

We applied four post-processing steps. First, predictions were restricted to the lobes named in the sentence, using the lung lobe masks provided with CT-RATE (generated with TotalSegmentator (7)) dilated by 32 voxels. Second, non-focal findings other than emphysema used a probability threshold of 0.9 instead of 0.5, because the model over-segmented these diffuse findings relative to the reference masks. Third, emphysema predictions were kept only in lung with attenuation below −910-910 HU, plus a three-voxel margin, because emphysema appears on CT as lung with abnormally low attenuation (26). Finally, connected components smaller than 5% of the largest component of the finding were removed. Post-processing settings were selected on the validation set and the public half of the ReXGrounding challenge test set (150 scans).

CTLink Ensemble

We also trained an ensemble of seven CTLink models as our entry for the ReXGrounding challenge comparison (Table 3). The ensemble averaged the probability maps of its members, which were selected on the public half of the ReXGrounding challenge test set (150 scans), as was possible for teams during the challenge’s public leaderboard phase. The members were five trainings of the CTLink recipe with different random seeds and two variants, one trained with non-focal findings sampled three times as often and one fine-tuned for 10 000 iterations without the non-focal findings of v1 scans. Post-processing was the same as for CTLink, except that, because averaging lowers the probabilities of the members, the mean probability map was thresholded at 0.35 instead of 0.5 (0.8 instead of 0.9 for non-focal findings other than emphysema), and predicted components smaller than 50 voxels were removed.

Evaluation Metrics

All metrics were computed with the official ReXGrounding challenge evaluator (24). Hit rate is the proportion of findings with a Dice score of at least 0.1 between the predicted and reference masks. The instance F1 score measures whether the separate instances of a finding are found, by matching connected components of the prediction one to one with the annotated reference instances (match at Dice score ≥\geq 0.2).

Comparison with Radiologists

In the inter-rater study of ReXGroundingCT (4), the findings of 50 cases from the test set were segmented a second time, independently, by other board-certified radiologists. On the 96 findings that the second segmentation also localized, we compared the agreement of CTLink with each segmentation with the agreement between the two segmentations (Appendix Appendix S1: Annotators).

Robustness Analysis

To test the robustness of CTLink to phrasing, we evaluated it on edited versions of the 582 test finding sentences. A large language model (GPT-6.1-sol; OpenAI) edited each sentence in five ways: (1) replacing terms, such as finding names or locations, with synonyms that radiologists typically use in chest CT reports, (2) replacing terms with standard radiology abbreviations, (3) freely rewording the whole sentence, (4) for findings in both lungs, splitting the sentence into a left-lung and a right-lung sentence, and (5) for findings in two or more lobes of one lung, splitting the sentence into one sentence per lobe. Details of the process are given in Appendix Appendix S2: Instance F1 Score of the Second Segmentation.

Error Analysis

Each finding in the ReXGrounding challenge test set that the CTLink ensemble missed (Dice score << 0.1) was assigned automatically to one of four error types: an empty prediction, partial overlap with the reference (Dice score above 0 but below 0.1), a prediction in the lobe of the reference without overlap (a different lesion in the right place), or a prediction outside that lobe (wrong location).

Statistical Analysis

95% CIs were estimated as percentile intervals from 2000 bootstrap replicates resampling scans. Models were compared with two-sided paired bootstrap tests on the same resampled scans, and P<.05P<.05 indicated a statistically significant difference. Models were implemented in Python (version 3.11), PyTorch (version 2.10), and nnU-Net (version 2.8.1), and statistical analyses were performed with NumPy (version 2.4).

Results

Dataset Characteristics

The v1 training set comprised 2989 scans from 2714 patients (mean age, 41.9 years ±\pm 14.3 [SD]; 56% male), the v2 training set 1134 scans from 1078 patients (41.5 years ±\pm 14.3; 57% male), the validation set 200 scans from 190 patients (39.5 years ±\pm 13.0; 56% male), and the test set 300 scans from 281 patients (42.8 years ±\pm 14.7; 58% male). Across the splits, 55%–60% of scans were acquired with Philips, 32%–36% with Siemens, and 8%–9% with Philips–Neusoft scanners. Most findings were focal: 6055 of 7652 (79%) in the v1 training set, 1704 of 1895 (90%) in the v2 training set, 329 of 381 (86%) in the validation set, and 509 of 582 (87%) in the test set (Table 1).

Ablation on the Validation Set

Table 2 shows the cumulative ablation. Most of the improvement over the published VoxTell model came from the training data: fine-tuning on the v1 scans with corrected masks raised the hit rate from 0.533 to 0.761, and adding the exhaustively annotated v2 scans raised it to 0.793 (P=.03P=.03) and the mean Dice score from 0.353 to 0.388 (P<.001P<.001). A comparison of the original and corrected v1 masks is shown in Appendix S3 and Table S1. Post-processing did not change the hit rate (P=.24P=.24) but raised the instance F1 score from 0.373 to 0.456 (P<.001P<.001), because it removed spurious components (instance precision, 0.293 to 0.460). Overall, CTLink localized 82% of validation findings, compared with 53% for VoxTell.

Challenge Test Set

Table 3 compares CTLink with the ReXGrounding challenge entries on all 300 test scans. A single CTLink model matched the challenge winner in hit rate (0.766 vs 0.768; P=.99P=.99) and mean Dice score (0.364 vs 0.375; P=.14P=.14) and had a higher instance F1 score (0.378 vs 0.341; P=.005P=.005), mainly because post-processing removed small spurious components. The CTLink ensemble had the highest hit rate (0.782) and instance F1 score (0.386) of all entries and the second-highest Dice score (0.373). Compared with the winner, the difference in instance F1 score was significant (P=.001P=.001), whereas the differences in hit rate (P=.41P=.41) and Dice score (P=.71P=.71) were not. Table S2 gives precision and recall, and Table S3 an analysis by patient sex and age (Appendix Appendix S4: Original versus Corrected v1 Masks).

Figure 2 compares the CTLink ensemble with the three top-ranked challenge entries and VoxTell on six test findings, and Figure 3 shows examples of successful CTLink ensemble segmentations.

Comparison with Radiologists

On the 50 test scans with a second segmentation (96 findings; Table 4), CTLink had a higher mean Dice score than the second segmentation (0.374 vs 0.321; P=.02P=.02), with a similar hit rate (0.823 vs 0.760; P=.20P=.20) and instance F1 score (0.399 vs 0.417; P=.60P=.60). The CTLink ensemble had a higher hit rate (0.854; P=.045P=.045) and mean Dice score (0.381; P=.01P=.01) than the second segmentation and a similar instance F1 score (0.412; P=.88P=.88). Of the 17 non-focal findings, the second segmentation localized 7 (41%), CTLink 11 (65%), and the CTLink ensemble 12 (71%). With the second segmentation as the reference, CTLink reached a mean Dice score of 0.381 (CTLink ensemble, 0.380), compared with 0.321 between the two segmentations (P=.008P=.008), and a similar hit rate (0.771 vs 0.760; P=.88P=.88). CTLink thus matched or exceeded inter-rater agreement.

Performance by Finding Category and Lesion Size

On the test set, CTLink localized focal findings more often than non-focal findings (Figure 4). The hit rate ranged from 0.73 for atelectasis and consolidation (nn = 89) to 0.84 for linear opacities (nn = 113) across focal categories and from 0.33 for septal thickening (nn = 9) to 0.60 for other non-focal findings (nn = 10) across non-focal categories. Among focal findings, the hit rate was similar for all reference volumes below 10 mL and higher for findings of 10 mL or more (0.87; Figure 4C), and only 16 of the 101 missed focal findings were smaller than 0.1 mL.

Robustness Analysis

Across the four edit types, CTLink was robust to the wording of the finding (Figure 5; Table S4). With synonyms or free rewording, its masks agreed closely with the original masks (median Dice score, 0.95 and 0.91), and the hit rate did not change significantly (P=.71P=.71 and P=.92P=.92). Only abbreviations lowered the hit rate, by 0.043 (P<.001P<.001). For the 290 findings in both lungs, requesting each lung separately and taking the union did not change the hit rate (0.762 vs 0.766; P=.89P=.89), and even without post-processing, 93% of the voxels predicted for a single lung lay in the requested lung. For the 31 findings in two or more lobes of one lung, requesting each lobe separately gave a hit rate of 0.806, compared with 0.710 for the original sentence (P=.07P=.07), and without post-processing, 78% of the voxels predicted for a single lobe lay in the requested lobe.

Error Analysis

Of the 127 test findings that the CTLink ensemble missed (examples in Figure 6), 3 (2%) had an empty prediction, 75 (59%) overlapped the reference partially, 22 (17%) had a prediction in the lobe of the reference without overlap, and 27 (21%) had a prediction in another location. Partial overlap was the most common error type for both focal (58 of 97, 60%) and non-focal findings (17 of 30, 57%). Some misses reflected the reference masks or a related abnormality rather than a failure to localize (Figure 6). For “Multiple pulmonary nodules in both lungs,” the reference masks extended beyond the nodules that the CTLink ensemble segmented. For “Small fluid loculations in the fissures,” it segmented the pleural fluid at the lung bases, and for “Emphysematous air densities in right anterior chest wall,” it segmented the pneumothorax along the right lung instead of the air in the chest wall.

Discussion

We developed CTLink, a text-prompted segmentation model for linking radiology report findings to 3D chest CT scans. To train it, we created ReXGroundingCTv2, which adds 1134 exhaustively annotated scans to ReXGroundingCT. CTLink localized 82% of findings on the ReXGroundingCT validation set, matched or exceeded radiologist inter-rater agreement on 50 test scans, and an ensemble of CTLink models had the highest hit rate and instance F1 score of all entries of the ReXGrounding challenge. At this level, CTLink could mark most reported findings on the scan for referring clinicians, and even most of the findings that the CTLink ensemble missed (75 of 127) overlapped the reference partially. The ablation showed that training data drove most of these gains: fine-tuning VoxTell on the v1 training set, which had been only a small part of its pretraining, gave by far the largest improvement, and adding the exhaustively annotated v2 training set the largest further gain in Dice score.

Our study had several limitations. First, non-focal findings, such as emphysema and bronchiectasis, remained the hardest part of the task, partly because the extent of a diffuse finding is hard to outline consistently. In the inter-rater study of ReXGroundingCT, the second radiologist segmentation reached a hit rate of only 0.41 for non-focal findings against the reference masks, compared with 0.84 for focal findings (4), and reader agreement on diffuse lung disease at CT is moderate even for visual assessment (27, 28). Second, all scans came from CT-RATE, a single-center collection of noncontrast chest CT scans, so performance on contrast-enhanced scans, other scanners, and other populations is unknown. Third, each reference mask was drawn by one annotator, although readers disagree even on which lung nodules are present (29). Fourth, the comparison with radiologists covered only 50 scans (96 findings). Finally, CTLink covers only findings of the lungs, airways, and pleura.

In conclusion, CTLink localized the findings described in chest CT reports at the level of agreement between radiologists, showing that report sentences can be linked automatically to the abnormalities they describe. Such links could let referring clinicians see each reported finding on the scan, make it easier to compare findings with prior examinations, and let readers verify AI-generated reports. Our results also showed that exhaustive and accurate annotation of the training data mattered more than changes to the training procedure, which should guide the design of future datasets. Future work should extend the approach to other body regions and imaging modalities, and establish consistent annotation of diffuse findings. We release CTLink and ReXGroundingCTv2 to support this work.

Appendix S1: Annotators

The v1 training masks were drawn by professional annotators or trained medical students and reviewed by board-certified radiologists (4). The final annotations of the 1134 ReXGroundingCTv2 training scans were made by board-certified radiologists for 887 scans (78.2%), by radiology residents for 226 scans (19.9%), and by medical students for 21 scans (1.9%). All reviewers were board-certified radiologists, who could accept, edit, or reject an annotation. All 21 scans finalized by medical students and 96 of the 226 scans finalized by radiology residents were reviewed, so the annotations of 1004 scans (88.5%) were drawn or reviewed by a board-certified radiologist.

Appendix S2: Instance F1 Score of the Second Segmentation

Table 4 gives an instance F1 score of 0.417 for the second segmentation, whereas the inter-rater study of ReXGroundingCT reported 0.47 for the same masks (4). The difference comes from how instances are defined. The inter-rater study of ReXGroundingCT used the instances as the radiologists drew them. The challenge evaluator, which we used for every model and for the second segmentation, instead counts each connected component of a mask as one instance, so a lesion drawn with disconnected parts counts as several instances. Scoring all masks with the evaluator keeps CTLink and the radiologists comparable. Hit rate and Dice score do not depend on instances and are the same under both definitions.

Appendix S3: Robustness Analysis

The rewritten sentences were generated with GPT-6.1-sol (version 2026-09-29; OpenAI) and checked with GPT-6-astra (version 2026-09-03; OpenAI), with the prompts in Figures S1 to S3. For each of the 582 test findings, the model wrote (1) a version in which terms were replaced only with synonyms that radiologists commonly write in their place with exactly the same meaning (for example, “Dependent atelectasis in both lower lobe basal segments” became “Dependent atelectasis in bilateral lower lobe basal segments”), (2) a version with standard radiology abbreviations (such as RUL, GGO, and bilat.), and (3) a version freely reworded as another radiologist would write it. (4) It also judged whether the finding involved both lungs (307 findings) and, if so, wrote a left-lung and a right-lung sentence that kept the finding type, the descriptors that apply to every lesion, and the locations on that side (for example, “Scarring in the apical segments of the upper lobes of both lungs” became “Scarring in the apical segment of the left upper lobe” and “Scarring in the apical segment of the right upper lobe”). (5) In the same way, it decided which lobes the finding involved, as stated or clearly implied by the sentence, and, for a finding in two or more lobes, wrote one sentence per lobe (34 findings involved two or more lobes of one lung).

A sentence was analyzed only if the check accepted it and it differed from the original. This left 297 of the 582 synonym sentences (the other 285 had no such synonym), 540 of the 582 abbreviated sentences, all 582 freely reworded sentences, and the left-lung and right-lung sentences of 290 of the 307 bilateral findings. For the lobe split, all lobe sentences were accepted for 31 of the 34 findings.

Each sentence was segmented by CTLink with the same post-processing. The lobe restriction used the lobes named in the sentence given, so it did not apply to abbreviated lobe names. CTLink without post-processing shows the effect of the network alone. Agreement was the Dice score between the masks predicted for the original and the reworded sentence (1 when both were empty). For bilateral findings, the left-lung and right-lung predictions were combined into one mask by taking their union and scored with the challenge evaluator against the reference mask of the finding. The side of each predicted voxel was that of the nearer lung in the TotalSegmentator lobe masks. The predictions for a finding’s lobe sentences were combined in the same way, and the lobe of each predicted voxel was that of the nearest TotalSegmentator lobe. 95% CIs and PP values are from paired bootstrap tests resampling scans (2000 replicates).

Appendix S4: Original versus Corrected v1 Masks

To measure the effect of the corrected v1 masks, VoxTell was fine-tuned on the v1 scans twice, once with the original and once with the corrected v1 masks, with the same recipe and random seed. Both models were evaluated at their final checkpoint on the 200 validation scans without post-processing (Table S1).

Appendix S5: Performance by Patient Sex and Age

Patient sex and age were taken from the CT-RATE metadata (sex per patient, age at the scan). Both were available for all 300 test scans. On the 300 ReXGrounding challenge test scans, CTLink had a lower hit rate (0.740 vs 0.802) and instance F1 score (0.367 vs 0.395) in men than in women, but the differences were not statistically significant (P=.09P=.09 and P=.30P=.30), and the mean Dice score was the same in both (0.364; P=.99P=.99; Table S3). In patients 60 years or older, CTLink had a lower hit rate (0.748 vs 0.799) and mean Dice score (0.355 vs 0.396) than in those younger than 40 years, but the differences were also not statistically significant (P=.30P=.30 and P=.22P=.22). Its instance F1 score was also lower in patients 60 years or older (0.337 vs 0.413; P=.03P=.03), who had more findings per scan (2.6 vs 1.8).
