Evaluating Pretrained Language Semantics for Structured EHR Prediction Tasks

Ashvin Gupta Brendan Delaney Alessandra Russo

ashvin.gupta19@imperial.ac.uk brendan.delaney@imperial.ac.uk a.russo@imperial.ac.uk

Abstract

Structured Electronic Health Records (EHR) models typically treat each clinical concept as a dataset-specific token, discarding the naturallanguage semantics associated with the event. As a result, relationships between concepts must largely be learned from EHR co-occurrence, without directly exploiting any prior semantic knowledge. We investigate how pretrained causal language models can be adapted for longitudinal EHR prediction by developing tEHR. Here, we represent events with their natural language description and use this text stream to apply EHR-adaptive continued pretraining, followed by task-specific discriminative fine-tuning using LoRA, utilising the LLM as a longitudinal patient-encoder. We evaluate this approach across four prediction tasks on the MIMIC-IV dataset and pancreatic cancer prediction on the CPRD dataset at 1–12 month horizons, with tEHR using the Mistral-7B-v0.3 backbone achieving the strongest overall performance. Ablations show that performance declines when clinical descriptions are removed or misaligned, when numerical values are omitted, and when either stage of adaptation is removed, while explicit elapsed-time descriptions provide little additional benefit. Analysis of the learned patient representations further reveals clinically coherent structure associated with distinct patterns of disease risk and clinical history.

1. Introduction

Electronic Health Records (EHRs) provide longitudinal records of diagnoses, medications, procedures, laboratory measurements, and other clinical events. Transformer-based architectures have achieved strong performance across tasks including early pancreatic cancer detection (Placido et al., 2023; Steinberg et al., 2023) and ICU outcome prediction (Harutyunyan et al., 2019), with recent work extending to generative sequence models (Kraljevic et al., 2023; Wornow et al., 2023; Renc et al., 2024; Shmatko et al., 2025; Rajamohan et al., 2025; Waxler et al., 2025), state-space models (Fallahpour et al., 2024), and prompt-based prediction (Chandak et al., 2026).

Despite their architectural differences, these approaches largely rely on each clinical event being expressed as unique tokens. Consequently, models must learn representations and relationships between concepts from their EHR co-occurrence, and cannot exploit any prior semantic knowledge. These clinical concepts are recorded using a range of coding systems, including SNOMED-CT (Stearns et al., 2001), ICD (World Health Organization), and Read Codes (Chisholm, 1990), with the choice of vocabulary varying across healthcare systems. between sites and evolve over time as coding practices and terminologies change. Consequently, the same underlying clinical concept may be represented by multiple codes across datasets, while the overall vocabulary can contain thousands of possible concepts: Non-Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus is represented by 768 out of > 360 , 000 active concepts in SNOMED-CT (NHS England Primary Care Domain, 2025). This heterogeneity often requires ontology mapping, code collapsing, or vocabulary refinement before modelling (Wang et al., 2024; Makarov and Lipkovich, 2025), creating a dataset-specific vocabulary and model. Transferring a model to a new dataset therefore requires mapping clinical codes to the expected vocabulary of the model, which can lead to information loss. Furthermore, models take different approaches to representing the time intervals between events or continuous values of recordings (Guo et al., 2026; Attrach et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026). These preprocessing requirements make structured-EHR modelling difficult to reuse consistently across datasets and healthcare systems.

Replacing symbolic clinical codes with their naturallanguage descriptions provides a direct way to exploit the semantic knowledge encoded in pretrained language models and avoid these preprocessing issues. Recent work suggests that these representations can improve structured EHR prediction, but it remains unclear how pretrained language semantics are beneficial for longitudinal modelling and how the language model should be incorporated into the prediction pipeline. Prompt-based approaches present patient histories directly to an LLM, either without parameter updates (Zhu et al., 2024) or with supervised fine-tuning (Shoham and Rappoport, 2024), treating prediction primarily as a language-generation problem. Other work uses pretrained language models as representation encoders. Event-level embedding approaches encode each clinical event and pass these through a separate longitudinal aggregator, typically a Transformer, for the final prediction (Hur et al., 2024; Park et al., 2025). Patient-level embedding approaches encode the full patient trajectory into a single representation that is subsequently used by a lightweight classifier, using either clinically pretrained encoders (Munoz-Farre et al., 2022) or general-purpose embedding-specific models (Hegselmann et al., 2026). These studies establish that natural-language representations can provide useful inductive bias for clinical prediction tasks, but leave unresolved how much performance comes from the pretrained semantics themselves.

To investigate these questions, we develop tEHR, a two-stage framework applicable to causal LLMs. tEHR converts structured clinical events into a natural-language event stream, applies continued pretraining on unlabelled EHR trajectories, and then performs task-specific discriminative fine-tuning using the LLM as the longitudinal patient-encoder. We use this framework to study which aspects of pretrained language representations and downstream adaptation are responsible for predictive performance in structured EHR tasks.

Our contributions are:

  1. We present tEHR, a framework for adapting pretrained autoregressive LLMs to structured EHR prediction through continued pretraining on longitudinal patient trajectories followed by taskspecific discriminative fine-tuning.

  2. We investigate how pretrained language semantics contribute to EHR prediction through controlled ablations of lexical grounding, model capacity, and adaptation, alongside comparisons with alternative semantic representations and their computational trade-offs.

  3. We demonstrate strong predictive performance across four MIMIC-IV tasks and pancreatic cancer prediction in CPRD, showing that tEHR can be independently applied across distinct healthcare settings and clinical coding systems.

2.3. Two-Stage Training Framework

The tEHR framework adapts a causal LLM in two stages. First, the LLM is fine-tuned on the unlabelled patient trajectories through continued pretraining. The second involves discriminative fine-tuning with a prediction head on top of a specific hidden representation for the downstream task of interest. Both stages use Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) (Hu et al., 2021; Schulman and Lab, 2025) applied to the self-attention and linear projection layers. For a pre-trained weight matrix W 0 ∈ Rd×k , LoRA represents the update ∆ W as the product of two low-rank matrices B ∈ Rd×r and A ∈ Rr×k , such that the modified forward pass is: h = ( W 0 +∆ W ) x = ( W 0 +α rBA)x,where_α_is the scaling constant and r is the rank. During both stages of training, W 0 remains frozen while only A and B are updated, substantially reducing the number of trainable parameters while retaining the pretrained representations of the backbone model.

3. Experiments

3.1. Datasets and Prediction Tasks

MIMIC-IV: We use MIMIC-IV version 3.1 (Johnson et al., 2024), a large critical-care dataset containing events recorded during hospital and intensive-care admissions. We assess four binary prediction tasks: ICU mortality, ICU readmission, hospital mortality, and 30-day hospital readmission. MIMIC-IV contains clinical concepts represented primarily using ICD and NDC coding systems. In our processed dataset, this corresponds to roughly 15 k unique medical codes. It provides a high-acuity evaluation setting characterised by dense event sequences and comparatively short prediction horizons.

CPRD: The Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) (Wolf et al., 2019) is a private UK primarycare database containing over 16 million patients, accessible only via a secure environment. Its clinical events are represented using Read Codes. Due to the heterogeneous nature of primary care coding practices in the UK, this dataset contains over 241 k unique medical codes. We construct a task-specific cohort for pancreatic cancer prediction. Eligible pancreatic cancer cases are matched to up to five controls on sex, birth year within ± 3 years, and general-practice region, resulting in 73 , 732 patients with up to 10 years of preceding clinical history (Appendix 6.2). This design focuses evaluation on clinically comparable cases and controls rather than the highly imbalanced full CPRD population. To assess prediction at different lead times, patient histories are right-censored at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months before the index date.

3.2. Baselines

We group our baselines into structured code-based models and text-based prediction approaches.

Structured Code-Based Models: XGBoost which is trained on bag-of-codes frequency features. Transformer Encoder following Placido et al. (2023), is trained on the corresponding symbolic event sequences with an analogous pretraining and supervised fine-tuning procedure. CLMBR-T (Wornow et al., 2023), is a 141M-parameter structured-EHR Transformer pretrained on 2.57 million patients. Applying CLMBR-T to our datasets requires mapping the dataset codes to the model’s vocabulary. Using the public MIMIC-IV-to-OMOP mapping pipeline (OHDSI), 79.4% of event occurrences are mapped.

3.3. Ablations

All ablations are conducted on the MIMIC-IV tasks because computational restrictions in the CPRD secure environment prevented an equivalent sweep on CPRD. We investigate four questions: (1) which stages of the tEHR training procedure are necessary, (2) which components of the textualised EHR representation contribute predictive information, (3) how sensitive performance is to backbone choice and model capacity, and (4) whether pretrained language semantics can be incorporated through alternative event representations.

Two-stage training framework. We evaluate three training variants to disentangle the contributions of domain-adaptive continued pretraining and task-specific adaptation. (1) we remove Stage 1 and initialise directly from the base LLM, applying the same supervised LoRA adaptation and linear classification head used in the full model. (2) we retain Stage 1 but freeze the adapted backbone during downstream training, optimising only the linear classification head. (3) we freeze the initial unadapted base LLM and train only the classification head. Together, these variants separate the contribution of unlabelled EHR adaptation, task-specific backbone adaptation, and generic pretrained representations.

Clinical information representation. We next test which components of the textualised EHR representation contribute to predictive performance using controlled perturbations. In the hashed-concept condition, each clinical concept is replaced by a deterministic, unique, and semantically meaningless identifier, preserving concept identity and recurrence structure

while removing lexical meaning. In the scrambledconcept condition, concept descriptions are deterministically permuted within event categories for the events that occurred for that patient, preserving plausible medical language and broad event type while breaking the correspondence between each concept and its true clinical meaning. In the no-time condition, explicit textualised elapsed-time intervals are removed while chronological event order is preserved. In the no-value condition, numerical measurement values are removed while retaining the identity of the corresponding laboratory or vital-sign event. Across all conditions, demographics, hospital-transfer events and event ordering are preserved, and only the targeted component of the representation is modified.

Backbone and model capacity. We evaluate the full two-stage tEHR framework using Mistral-7Bv0.3, Qwen3-8B, and Qwen3-0.6B to assess robustness across LLM families and sensitivity to model capacity. To distinguish the contribution of pretrained lexical information from model capacity, we additionally construct a capacity-controlled variant using the Mistral7B backbone, Mistral-7B atomic codes , replacing natural-language event descriptions with atomic clinical-code tokens. The vocabulary is extended with the clinical codes used by the structured baselines, whose embeddings are newly initialised. This variant follows the same two-stage training framework as tEHR, preserving backbone capacity and the twostage adaptation procedure while replacing pretrained lexical representations with newly initialised code embeddings.

Alternative semantic representations. We investigate three alternative approaches to incorporating pretrained language semantics into longitudinal EHR prediction. First, LLM-initialised Transformer uses a code-based Transformer whose clinical-code embeddings are initialised from pretrained LLM representations of their corresponding natural-language descriptions, Hur et al. (2024). Second, Event LLM embeddings , following Park et al. (2025), represents individual clinical events using frozen LLM embeddings, which are passed to a trainable Transformer for longitudinal prediction. Third, LLM projection projects pretrained event-level LLM embeddings into the input embedding space of a longitudinal LLM. The latter two approaches use task-specific supervised training without the autoregressive next-token continued pretraining stage employed by tEHR. 3.4. Representation Analysis and Clinical Characterisation

To investigate the patient representations learned by tEHR, we extract the 4 , 096-dimensional [PREDICT] embeddings from the test set of the 1-month pancreatic cancer model. We focus on this setting because it provides a strong predictive signal and pancreatic cancer has well-characterised clinical risk factors that facilitate interpretation of the learned representations. Embeddings are first ℓ 2-normalised and reduced to 10 dimensions using UMAP (McInnes et al., 2020). We use UMAP rather than PCA to preserve potentially nonlinear local neighbourhood structure in the learned representations, rather than prioritising linear explained variance and allow us to use densitybased clustering. HDBSCAN is applied in this 10dimensional space to identify dense groups of patients without specifying the number of clusters in advance. A separate two-dimensional UMAP projection is used solely for visualisation and does not affect cluster assignment. Clusters are characterised by their size, pancreatic cancer prevalence, categorical-event enrichment, and distributions of continuous laboratory measurements and hyperparameters are selected using silhouette scores on the validation set and fixed be-

fore test-set analysis (Appendix 6.4). For categorical events, enrichment is defined as the prevalence of an event within a cluster relative to its prevalence in the full population. This analysis is intended to assess whether the task-adapted representation organises patients into clinically coherent phenotypes beyond the binary case-control label.

4. Results

tEHR-Mistral achieved the strongest overall performance across all four MIMIC-IV tasks (Table 1) and all pancreatic cancer prediction horizons in CPRD (Figure 2). On MIMIC-IV, the strongest competing baselines were the code-based Transformer and finetuned CLMBR-T, while zero-shot prompting showed near chance-level discrimination on several tasks. On CPRD, tEHR also outperformed all baselines, with XGBoost being the strongest competitor. Performance declined across all models as the prediction horizon increased, reflecting the greater difficulty of earlier risk prediction.

The ablations demonstrate the importance of both adaptation stages (Figure 3). Removing continued pretraining reduced AUROC across all four MIMIC-IV tasks, while restricting downstream adaptation to only the linear head led to further deterioration. 5. Discussion

We present tEHR, a framework for adapting pretrained autoregressive LLMs to EHR prediction tasks. tEHR can in principle be applied to different autoregressive LLMs and relies on clinical events being repre-

sented with their natural-language descriptions rather than as symbolic tokens, allowing the model to reuse semantic information learnt during large-scale pretraining. tEHR requires simple data preprocessing, avoids the construction of a model-specific vocabulary and can still be applied to large models through parameter-efficient fine-tuning with LoRA. However, the other LLM-based experiments show that simply introducing pretrained semantic embeddings does not recover the performance of full textualisation. Neither zero-shot, description-initialised code embeddings nor event-level LLM representations matched tEHR, suggesting that the manner in which semantic information is incorporated into longitudinal modelling is also important.

The ablations provide a possible explanation for the success of tEHR. Continued pretraining adapts the model to the distribution and sequential structure of the EHR data, while task-specific fine-tuning further shapes the representation for the downstream prediction task. The frozen base-model ablation assesses the predictive utility of generic pretrained representations without EHR-specific adaptation. The improvements obtained through continued pretraining and task-specific fine-tuning suggest that both stages contribute to learning effective longitudinal patient representations. The lexical ablations further demonstrate the importance of correctly grounding clinical events in natural-language. In hashed-concept , the model is unable to identify the semantic meaning of

the events occurring and performs worse. Similarly, the scrambled-concept ablation indicates that it is not sufficient for the input to contain plausible clinical language but that the correspondence between a clinical event and its surrounding context matters. Together, these perturbations suggest that the model benefits from the correspondence between clinical concepts and their pretrained lexical representations. This is further supported by the capacity-controlled Mistral-7B experiment, where replacing natural-language descriptions with atomic clinical-code tokens substantially reduced performance despite retaining the same backbone and two-stage training framework. Together, these findings suggest that pretrained lexical knowledge contributes to tEHR’s performance beyond model capacity alone.

A possible explanation for the drop in performance when the clinical values are removed is that the model can no longer directly distinguish normal from abnormal measurement values. This is not the same case for time, consistent with previous work (Attrach et al., 2025) and suggesting that chronological ordering may therefore capture much of the required temporal information. Performance also varied across pretrained backbones. Differences between Mistral-7B and Qwen3-8B, despite similar parameter counts, suggest that pretraining differences may influence downstream performance, although these experiments do not isolate their source. The weaker performance of Qwen3-0.6B further indicates sensitivity to model capacity. These performance improvements come with a computational trade-off as tEHR was an expensive method for both training and inference. More compact semantic representation strategies offered lower training costs, albeit with reduced predictive performance.

The CLMBR-T experiment illustrates a practical limitation of structured-EHR foundation models with fixed vocabularies. Although CLMBR-T performs strongly within its intended representation setting, applying it to our MIMIC-IV cohorts required mapping source concepts into the vocabulary expected by the model. Only 79.4% of event occurrences were successfully mapped, which may partly explain the remaining performance gap. We did not identify an equivalent mapping suitable for CPRD Read Codes, preventing a corresponding evaluation there. In contrast, tEHR operates on natural-language concept descriptions, avoiding explicit alignment to a pretrained symbolic vocabulary. An interesting future experiment would be to evaluate the cross-dataset transfer of a tEHR

model which would complement recent work by Ding et al. (2026) on unifying vocabularies across datasets. The CPRD experiments further evaluate the tEHR method on primary-care, which is a substantially different setting from MIMIC-IV. CPRD contains longitudinal primary-care data recorded across many practices and therefore results in a much larger and more heterogeneous clinical vocabulary. A common preprocessing step is to map codes to higher-level clinical concepts, which is manually intensive and can lead to incorrect medical timelines if there are mistakes. The strong performance of tEHR in this setting suggests that predictive models can be developed without extensive code simplification. The strong performance of XGBoost also shows that model complexity alone is not sufficient for strong prediction in this setting. The latent-space analysis provides additional evidence that the learned representation contains clinically meaningful structure. The pancreatic-cancerenriched region contains features associated with the disease, while other regions capture distinct inflammatory, metabolic, reproductive, and urological patterns. However, clustering does not establish causal mechanisms, and the identified groups may be sensitive to the choice of dimensionality-reduction and clustering parameters.

Several limitations remain. First, CPRD evaluation was restricted by the approved study protocol and computational constraints to pancreatic cancer prediction, with the full ablation suite therefore performed only on MIMIC-IV. Evaluation across additional diseases and prediction tasks would provide a stronger test of generality. Second, textualisation expands clinical events into multiple language-model tokens, increasing sequence length and computational cost. We therefore limit the LLM context window to 16K tokens and retain the most recent clinical history for longer trajectories (Appendix 6.6). Finally, further work could investigate cross-dataset transfer, retention of pretrained knowledge, and the interpretability of learned patient representations.

6.2. Dataset information

The Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) Aurum is a UK-based longitudinal primary care database derived from routinely collected electronic health records in general practice. CPRD contains detailed patient-level information including diagnoses, prescriptions, laboratory results, referrals, and demographic variables, with linkage options to additional datasets such as hospital admissions and mortality records. The database is broadly representative of the UK population.

CPRD pancreatic cancer cohort construction. Pancreatic cancer cases were identified from CPRD and assigned an index date corresponding to their recorded date of diagnosis. For each case, we sought up to five controls matched on sex, birth year within ± 3 years, and general-practice region. Controls were required to have no recorded pancreatic cancer diagnosis throughout their available follow-up, and patients who subsequently developed pancreatic cancer were not eligible to serve as controls. Each control was used at most once. For controls, the index date was defined as two years before their final recorded clinical event to reduce the likelihood of including patients with an undiagnosed pancreatic cancer near the end of follow-up. Consequently, matching was performed on birth year rather than age at index, and differences in age at index between cases and controls are expected because their index dates are defined differently. Matching was performed before the cohort was divided into training, validation, and test sets. Up to five eligible controls were selected for each case; cases were retained when fewer than five matching controls were available. Due to case-control matching, AUPRC values should not be interpreted as population screening precision.

6.3. Baselines and Ablations

XGBoost We implement XGBoost using bag-of-codes frequency features constructed from all discrete medical concepts observed in each patient’s available history. Each feature corresponds to a clinical code and records the number of times that code occurs before the task-specific prediction time. This provides a nonsequential structured-data baseline that retains event frequency but discards temporal ordering, continuous values, and relationships between events.

Transformer Encoder We implement a code-based Transformer encoder, similar to Placido et al. (2023), with 12 encoder layers, 8 attention heads, and a maximum context length of 2,048 events. The model operates directly on symbolic medical codes rather than natural-language descriptions, with each code becoming a unique token. Temporal information and continuous measurements are discretised into additional learned representations following the code-based preprocessing pipeline.

The encoder follows an analogous two-stage training procedure to tEHR. In Stage 1, it is pretrained on trajectories from the training split using masked language modelling with a masking probability of 15%; of selected tokens, 80% are replaced by the mask token, 10% by a random token, and 10% are left unchanged, following BERT (Devlin et al., 2019). In Stage 2, a linear classification head is added to the patient representation and the encoder is fine-tuned jointly for each downstream prediction task using binary cross-entropy. We train both stages for 50 epochs with early stopping on validation loss.

CLMBR-T We evaluate CLMBR-T (Wornow et al., 2023), a 141M-parameter structured-EHR Transformer pretrained on 2.57 million patients, on the four MIMIC-IV tasks. Because CLMBR-T operates over a predefined structured vocabulary, MIMIC-IV concepts were mapped using the public MIMIC-IV-to-OMOP pipeline (OHDSI). This mapped 79.4% of observed event occurrences, with unmapped events omitted from the input sequence. We evaluate two variants. In the frozen setting, CLMBR-T is used as a fixed patient representation and only a logistic-regression classification head is trained. In the fine-tuned setting, the pretrained model is additionally adapted to each downstream task before classification. Because the mapping is incomplete, these results should be interpreted as a transfer benchmark under partial vocabulary alignment rather than as a comparison under fully matched input representations.

Mistral-7B with Atomic Clinical-Code Tokens To assess whether tEHR’s performance can be explained by the capacity of the pretrained LLM rather than its use of NL event descriptions, we construct a capacitycontrolled variant using the same Mistral-7B backbone. Instead of textualising clinical codes, each unique medical concept is represented by an atomic token. We extend the Mistral vocabulary with the same clinical-code vocabulary used by the code-based Transformer baselines, with newly initialised embeddings for these codes.

The model follows the same two-stage training procedure as tEHR. During Stage 1, the LoRA adapters and newly introduced code embeddings are updated through autoregressive next-token continued pretraining on patient trajectories. During Stage 2, the same linear classification head is introduced, and the LoRA adapters and code embeddings are further optimised jointly with the classification head using binary cross-entropy. This preserves the pretrained backbone, model capacity, and adaptation procedure while replacing natural-language descriptions with learned symbolic code representations.

6.4. UMAP and HDBSCAN Implementation

Patient representations were analysed using UMAP followed by HDBSCAN clustering. Hyperparameters were selected on the validation set and then fixed before applying the analysis to the held-out test set. All embeddings were ℓ 2-normalised before dimensionality reduction.

Clustering. For hyperparameter selection, validation-set embeddings were reduced from 4 , 096 to 10 dimensions using UMAP. We searched over UMAP and HDBSCAN configurations, including n_neighbors = 10–50, min_cluster_size = 50–200, and min_samples = 10–40. Candidate configurations were required to produce 4–12 non-noise clusters with a noise fraction between 0.5% and 20%, and were ranked by silhouette score on the non-noise validation samples.

The selected configuration used UMAP with n_neighbors = 22, min_dist = 0 . 0, and Euclidean distance, followed by HDBSCAN with min_cluster_size = 127, min_samples = 12, Euclidean distance, and the eom cluster-selection method. These hyperparameters were then fixed and applied to embeddings from the held-out test set. HDBSCAN noise points were excluded from the clinical phenotype analysis.

Visualisation. For visualisation only, the test-set embeddings were independently projected to two dimensions using UMAP with n_neighbors = 15 and min_dist = 0 . 1. Cluster assignments from the 10-dimensional representation were overlaid on this projection; the two-dimensional projection was not used for clustering.

6.6. Tokenisation and context length

Textualising structured EHR events expands each clinical event into multiple language-model subword tokens. On MIMIC-IV training trajectories, we measured a median expansion of 8.41 Mistral tokens per event (Table 7). At this rate, tEHR’s 16,000-token context window corresponds to approximately 1,900 clinical events at the median expansion rate. This is close to, but slightly below, the 2,048-event context used by the code-based Transformer. Consequently, tEHR does not gain an advantage from access to longer longitudinal histories. For sufficiently long trajectories, the Transformer Encoder can retain somewhat more clinical events than tEHR. For shorter trajectories that fit within both context limits, both models receive the full available history.
