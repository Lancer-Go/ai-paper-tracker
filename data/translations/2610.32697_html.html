SP-Guide: Enhancing Aerial Vision-Language Navigation with Spatial Priors

Abstract — Aerial vision-language navigation (AVLN) requires a UAV to reach a language-described target from partial egocentric observations while reasoning over large outdoor scenes. Existing approaches typically fuse view-centered RGB observations and globally anchored history maps as generic visual embeddings, leaving cross-modal spatial alignment to be learned from sparse navigation supervision. We present SP-Guide, a transformer¬ based goal-prediction framework that addresses this weakness of alignment through a Spatial Prior Cross-Modal Encoder, an Instruction Enhanced Decoder, and a Goal Convergence Filter for terminal target refinement. Experiments implemented on the CityNav benchmark show that SP-Guide improves success rate over the representative baselines, with the largest gains on unseen environments. These results indicate that physically grounded spatial priors provide an effective inductive bias for robust AVLN. The code is available at https://github.com/Dongzhou-1996/SPGuide.

Index Terms —aerial vision-language navigation, spatial prior, multimodal transformer, geographic semantic maps

I. Introduction

In the Aerial vision-language navigation (AVLN) task, an unmanned aerial vehicle navigates based on natural-language guidance toward a target while perceiving the world through partial egocentric observations. This capability is relevant to urban inspection, emergency response, and low-altitude autonomous exploration, where an aerial agent must repeatedly connect local evidence to large-scale scene structure [1], [2]. Compared with indoor Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN), the aerial setting introduces substantially greater diffi¬ culties. First, viewpoints shift rapidly during flight, leading to limited visual overlap between consecutive frames and requir¬ ing the agent to robustly handle large motion discontinuities. Second, target objects are often distant and thus occupy only a small portion of the image, which demands fine-grained

feature extraction and multi-scale perception to reliably de¬ tect and localise them. Third, long-range spatial consistency must be maintained throughout the entire trajectory, as the agent needs to continuously integrate local observations into a coherent global representation for accurate path planning and goal reaching.

A central difficulty in AVLN is multimodal spatial mis¬ match. The agent reasons jointly over egocentric RGB/depth observations and a globally anchored semantic map. These modalities are complementary, but they do not share the same coordinate semantics: visual observation embeddings are viewcentered, whereas global map embeddings are world¬ centered. If they are fused as generic modality embeddings, the network must infer cross-modal correspondence, instruction relevance, and goal geometry simultaneously from sparse supervision. This ambiguity becomes especially severe when several regions are semantically plausible but only one is consistent with both the instruction and the global layout.

Terminal decision making introduces a second source of er¬ ror. In goal-prediction agents [2], the predicted progress score is often used as an approximate stop signal. When the progress estimate is noisy, the agent may stop too early, overshoot the target, or oscillate around the goal region. Consequently, better one-step prediction does not automatically translate into stronger closed-loop navigation.

To address these issues mentioned above, we present the SP-Guide method, a transformer-based AVLN framework that introduces spatially constrained cross-modal reasoning and more reliable terminal commitment. The method preserves standard preprocessing and pretrained modality encoders, but strengthens how local observations are aligned with the global map and how the final target is selected near the end of rollout.

This design keeps the comparison focused on representation and decision quality rather than on broad architectural replace¬

ment.

  • ExperimentalresultsshowthatSP-Guideconsistently outperforms conventional baselines on a large-scale aerial vision-language navigation benchmark, demonstrating the value of spatial-prior-guided reasoning for robust naviga¬ tion.

II. Related Work

A. Aerial Vision-Language Navigation

Vision-language navigation first gained traction in graphstructured indoor environments, where the agent typically moves over a discrete navigation graph and follows long natural-language route instructions [3]-[5], This formulation enabled rapid progress in instruction grounding, progress esti¬ mation, and imitation learning, but it also hides many low-level control difficulties behind oracle graph transitions. Continuous indoor VLN therefore emerged as an important next step. VLN-CE removes the navigation graph and requires agents to act in continuous 3D environments from RGB-D observations, establishing Seq2Seq and CMA as standard embodied base¬ lines [6]. Later work explored language-conditioned waypoint prediction [7] and explicit cross-modal map learning [8], showing that continuous VLN performance depends strongly on how local perception, spatial memory, and language are coupled. These indoor studies are important references for AVLN because they expose the same representation-to-action bottleneck, even though aerial navigation involves larger out¬ door scenes and more severe viewpoint changes.

Recent aerial benchmarks extend this line of work to geographically large outdoor scenes [1], [2], CityNav is es¬ pecially relevant because it provides a large-scale real-world urban AVLN dataset with natural-language descriptions, hu¬ man demonstration trajectories, and a geographic semantic map (GSM) that encodes stable top-down spatial cues and instruction-mentioned landmarks. This landmark-aware map representation improves long-range exploration efficiency by giving the agent geographically meaningful anchors rather than relying only on short-range egocentric evidence.

B. Spatial Priors in Vision Transformers

The Transformer was originally introduced for sequence transduction with multi-head self-attention and encoder¬ decoder processing [9], Its success motivated direct adaptation to vision, where ViT treats image patches as tokens and processes them with a standard Transformer encoder [10]. Subsequent variants improved the visual inductive bias of this

design: DeiT made ViT training more data efficient through attention-based distillation [11], while Swin Transformer intro¬ duced hierarchical shifted-window attention to recover locality and multi-scale structure [12]. These developments show that although attention provides flexible global interaction, effec¬ tive visual transformers often still require spatial structure to be injected through tokenization, locality, hierarchy, or positional bias.

A more focused line of work therefore reintroduces ge¬ ometry directly into token interaction. CPVT [13] replaces fixed positional embeddings with conditional positional encod¬ ings, while SP-ViT [14] and enhanced-spatial-prior attention modules explicitly bias token interaction with two-dimensional geometry [15]. Together, these studies show that transformer attention benefits from spatial priors rather than relying en¬ tirely on feature similarity.

Most spatial-prior transformers are designed for a single visual lattice. Their priors are typically defined over patch neighborhoods or relative offsets within one modality. That assumption is appropriate for image classification and dense prediction, but it is incomplete for AVLN with semantic maps. Our setting requires cross-modal grounding across two coordinate systems: a view-centered observation lattice and a globally anchored map lattice. SP-Guide extends spatialprior reasoning from within-modality locality to cross-modality spatial consistency by constraining attention with distances measured in a shared physical frame.

III. Method

A. Task Formulation

We consider aerial vision-language navigation (AVLN) in a large-scale urban environment. At the beginning of each episode, a UAV agent receives a natural-language instruction L that describes a target location or destination. The agent starts from an initial pose (po, V’o). where po G R2 denotes the planar position and u’o denotes the heading angle. During navigation, the agent does not observe the full environment; at each step t, it receives a partial observation of and selects a discrete action at G A conditioned on the instruction and observation history.

Let g* G R2 denote the ground-truth target position in the global coordinate frame. Executing the policy for at most T steps produces a trajectory of poses, observations, and actions. The objective is to generate an action sequence that follows the instruction and drives the UAV to g*. An episode is considered successful if the final UAV position lies within a predefined tolerance radius e of the target.
