InstructReplay: Replaying Assistant Turns to Preserve General Behavior During Factual Fine-Tuning

Xiaofeng Shi∗ Qian Kou Hua Zhou Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence (BAAI)

Abstract

Fine-tuning an instruction-tuned language model on new facts often erodes its general abilities. We find that standard fine-tuning lowers instruction-following, reasoning and coding benchmarks by 35 points on average, and that these losses track a collapse in answer length: fine-tuned models at least halve their responses, and the further a response drifts from the initial model’s length, the more often it fails. We propose InstructReplay: the initial model answers a set of general instructions once, and during fine-tuning the model is kept close to the initial model on these answers. Across three model families and two domains, InstructReplay reduces the average decline on five general benchmarks from 28.1 to 2.4 points and keeps answer length close to the initial model’s, while learning the new facts as well as standard fine-tuning. It balances learning and retention better than seven adaptation methods in all six settings. Controlled comparisons attribute the gain to replaying the model’s own answers to general instructions rather than to the form of the loss.

1 Introduction

Practitioners often adapt an instruction-tuned language model to a domain by fine-tuning it on question–answer pairs that state new facts. The facts are learned, but at a cost elsewhere. Finetuned on recent biomedical articles, SmolLM3-3B answers 21 points more of the new questions correctly, yet its instruction-following accuracy on IFEval falls from 71% to 9%. Fine-tuning is known to be an inefficient way of adding knowledge (Gekhman et al., 2024; Ovadia et al., 2024). We study how to preserve general assistant behavior during closed-book factual fine-tuning.

Their responses shrink toward the short domain answers they were trained on, and the more a method shortens responses, the more accuracy it loses (§3). The adapted models thus stop behaving as the assistants they were, even though likelihood-based probes change little.

Replaying an earlier model’s predictions is a standard remedy for such losses (Li and Hoiem, 2016; Buzzega et al., 2020). A distillation penalty, however, constrains the model only at the prefixes where it is applied, so the choice of replay inputs decides which behavior is protected. Recent selfgenerated replay for instruction-tuned models samples from a beginning-of-sequence (BOS) token, outside the chat template (Marek et al., 2026), and context-free synthetic data likewise avoids external prompts (Bansal and Sanghavi, 2025). Selfsynthesized rehearsal does use instruction contexts, but it synthesizes and refines them and trains on the refined outputs (Huang et al., 2024). What has not been isolated is which ingredients of replay matter: where the reference model is matched, whose responses are replayed, and in what form.

The initial model answers 9,600 general instructions once, and during domain training the model matches the frozen initial model’s full next-token distributions on these saved responses. We compare it with seven domain-adaptation methods and with three controlled variants. 2 Related Work

Learning new facts by fine-tuning. Facts that a model does not already know are learned more slowly than known ones and, once learned, increase hallucination (Gekhman et al., 2024), and retrieval augmentation outperforms unsupervised

fine-tuning for knowledge injection (Ovadia et al., 2024). We take closed-book fine-tuning as given and study its cost to general behavior.

Preserving earlier behavior. Parameter-space penalties such as EWC protect weights deemed important for earlier tasks (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017); low-rank adaptation (Hu et al., 2022) learns less and forgets less than full fine-tuning (Biderman et al., 2024). Function-space methods instead match an earlier model’s outputs: Learning without Forgetting distills the old model on new-task inputs (Li and Hoiem, 2016), Dark Experience Replay stores logits alongside rehearsed examples (Buzzega et al., 2020), and label annealing distills the initial model on the fine-tuning data itself (Yang et al., 2024b). Rehearsing a small share of earlier tasks preserves abilities in sequentially finetuned language models (Scialom et al., 2022). Forgetting after fine-tuning often reflects a shift in how the model responds rather than a loss of capability (Kotha et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2025), and updates driven by a model’s own on-policy outputs forget less than supervised fine-tuning on external targets (Shenfeld et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026). Our Domain-KL baseline places the distillation term on domain answers, as label annealing does, and therefore isolates the choice of context.

Responses become short, and shortening predicts the loss. Median responses to IFEval prompts shrink from 190 to 20 words, and MATH500 solutions from 242 to 42; Table 2 shows an example in which a request for a letter receives a single sentence. This matches the training data: domain answers average about 30 tokens, while the initial models’ answers to general instructions average about 340. Spearman’s ρ is 0.77 on IFEval, 0.81 on MATH-500 and 0.84 on BIG-Bench Hard (Appendix J).

The losses are not only word-count failures.

Naive SFT’s IFEval pass rate falls by 11 points on length constraints but by 55 points or more on constraints that require structure or specific content, such as formats, required openings and endings, and combined constraints (Appendix Table 25). Among questions that M 0 answers correctly, the failure rate of adapted models is lowest, below 20%, when a response stays within 25% of M 0’s length, and far higher when it is much shorter or much longer (Figure 2b). What predicts failure is thus departure from M 0’s response, of which shortening is the most common form.

Hypothesis. Fitting short domain answers shifts the model’s behavior in assistant turns toward terse, direct answers, which breaks instruction following and chain-of-thought formatting; knowledge probed by likelihood is affected far less. If the hypothesis holds, a retention penalty should be most effective when it is applied in the contexts where assistant behavior is exercised.

4.2 Why instruction contexts?

Forward KL at a fixed prefix h has logit gradient pθ ( · | h ) − p 0( · | h ). It pulls the student toward

the reference only at the prefixes in the replay set and affects other contexts only through shared parameters, so the choice of prefixes determines what is preserved. Near θ 0, the replay loss is a quadratic penalty whose curvature is built only from the logit Jacobians at the replayed prefixes (Appendix A). It resists parameter changes that alter the model’s outputs in those contexts and leaves other directions unconstrained. Domain-answer prefixes, as in Domain-KL, lie in exactly the contexts that LD is changing. BOS-conditioned samples are freeform continuations generated without the chat template, which an instruction-tuned model rarely encounters at inference. Responses to general instructions under the native template place the constraint on assistant turns spanning instruction following, reasoning, mathematics, code and translation. These are the contexts in which general benchmarks and users query the model, and in which §3 located the damage. Section 6.2 tests this ordering, comparing instruction and BOS contexts at a matched response-token budget.

We use soft targets because they exert no pull where the student already agrees with the reference, whereas a sampled token yields a nonzero gradient at every prefix (Appendix A). A token sampled from M 0 still gives an unbiased estimate of the forward-KL gradient, so the two targets differ in variance rather than in what they preserve, and we expect the choice of prefixes, not the form of the target, to matter most. Section 6.2 compares soft and sampled-token targets at the same replay coefficient.

5 Experimental Setup

5.1 Models, domains and replay data

We adapt SmolLM3-3B (Bakouch et al., 2025), Qwen3-4B (Qwen Team, 2025) and Phi-4-miniinstruct (Microsoft, 2025); each domain adaptation starts from the unchanged checkpoint M 0. Each domain provides 9,600 training question–answer pairs and 512 evaluation questions that ask about taught facts in new wording, without a source passage. CommonCorpus energy/engineering (PleIAs, 2024) draws on technical documents; PMC biomedical (National Library of Medicine, 2026) uses CC BY 4.0 articles published online between May and August 2026, after all three models were released. Appendix B describes construction, examples and the extent to which some questions cue part of the answer.

4

The replay collection holds 9,600 prompts from UltraData-SFT (OpenBMB, 2026), split equally across instruction following, academic tasks, mathematics, code and translation and selected without reference to model scores. Each model samples its own responses (temperature 1, at most 512 tokens); the dataset’s own assistant answers are used only by the Gold replay control (§5.3).

5.2 Measuring acquisition and forgetting

Acquisition. Three judges from different model families (Qwen3.8-27B, Gemma-4-31B-it, GPTOSS-20B) compare each greedy answer with a source-supported fact card; an answer is confirmed when at least two judges validly find all core facts supported. With C and C0 confirmations for the adapted model and M 0 out of ND = 512 questions, acquisition is A = 100( C − C0 )/ ND . Unresolved and ineligible questions stay in the denominator; on CommonCorpus, a fixed fact-card rule makes 374 of the 512 questions eligible (Appendix B.4).

Forgetting. We measure general ability on fixed subsets of IFEval (Zhou et al., 2023), MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021a), MATH-500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021b; Lightman et al., 2024), BBH (Suzgun et al., 2023) and HumanEval+ (Liu et al., 2023), using the same questions across models and methods. Subset sizes and selection procedures are detailed in Appendix B.5. We summarize the trade-off by S = A − F , but report A and F throughout and show in §6.3 that the conclusions hold for any weighting that puts forgetting at more than 0.31 times acquisition.

5.3 Baselines and controls

Domain-adaptation baselines. Naive SFT and LoRA-SFT train on the gold answers; SelfRewrite, SDFT and RAFT change the domain supervision; DFT reweights target tokens; and Domain-KL adds our KL term ( λ = 0 . 25) on domain-answer prefixes. Appendix C and Table 13 give their implementations.

Replay controls. Three controls keep InstructReplay’s λ = 0 . 25, 1:1 ratio and schedule and change one component at a time. BOS replay changes the context: it applies the KL to samples generated from a beginning-of-sequence token without the chat template, the sampling used by Marek et al. (2026). This comparison eval-

uates BOS sampling under our training protocol, not their full method; Appendix H lists the configuration differences. Hard changes the target: it keeps InstructReplay’s saved responses but replaces the KL with next-token cross-entropy. Both match InstructReplay’s number of response tokens sequence by sequence. Gold replay changes the author of the replayed answers: it keeps Hard’s prompts and loss but trains on the collection’s own assistant answers, truncated to 512 tokens.

Training. Every method runs 300 optimizer steps with 32 domain sequences per step, so all methods see the same domain examples and are evaluated at the same step; replay methods add 32 replay sequences per step. Full-parameter methods use a constant learning rate of 10_−5 and LoRA uses 10−_4 (Appendix C). The replay ratio, λ = 0 . 25 and the schedule were tuned on CommonCorpus with SmolLM3 and Qwen3 and transferred unchanged to Phi-4-mini and to PMC.

5.4 Study design and uncertainty

PMC tests the same recipe, unchanged, on a second domain. For question-sampling uncertainty we use a paired cluster bootstrap that resamples domain questions by shared source and general-benchmark questions within benchmark strata, with Bonferroni correction over all 36 pairs among the eight methods and M 0 in each setting (Appendix F).

6 Results

6.1 Main comparison

Instruction-context replay removes most forgetting and keeps acquisition on par with standard fine-tuning. InstructReplay has the highest S in all six settings (Table 3). The gain comes almost entirely from reduced forgetting. Averaged over the six settings, InstructReplay’s general decline is 2.44 points against 28.07 for Naive SFT, and it stays below 4.7 in every setting, whereas the lowest-forgetting baseline in each setting loses at least 3.73. No general benchmark loses more than 3 points on average under InstructReplay, whereas standard fine-tuning loses 28 or more on each generative benchmark. Its acquisition is within 1.95 points of Naive SFT or higher in every setting, and its average of 15.49 is second only to RAFT’s 16.15. InstructReplay Pareto-dominates 35 of the 42 baseline points in the A – F plane (Figure 3); each exception acquires at most 3.1 points more but forgets at least 2.33 points more.

6.2 Where to constrain and what to match

The location of the constraint drives retention. Table 4 holds the objective fixed and moves it. Placing the KL on domain-answer prefixes barely helps: IFEval still falls by 43 points and responses remain a fifth of their original length. Placing it on BOSconditioned samples, which are generated without the chat template, recovers about two thirds of the IFEval loss. Placing it on the model’s own answers to general instructions recovers almost all of it, and restores response length to 85% of M 0’s. Acquisition stays between 14.0 and 15.7 points throughout, so the differences in S come from forgetting. At an identical response-token budget, InstructReplay has higher S than BOS replay in all six settings, by 1.28 to 11.05 points, and lower F in each (Appendix H).

7 Analysis

Replay source. The diagnosis in §3 ties the loss of general ability to responses that drift away from M 0’s. Replay counters this drift best when its targets are M 0’s own responses in assistant turns. Domain-answer prefixes cover only short factual answers, and BOS samples lie outside the chat template. The dataset’s answers are assistant turns in a different style, so fitting them moves the model away from M 0 in another direction (Appendix H). Gold replay keeps M 0’s MATH-500 response length yet loses 4.3 points there, against 0.2 for InstructReplay, so matching length alone does not preserve accuracy. Updates driven by a model’s own outputs have likewise been found to forget less than training on external targets (Shenfeld et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026).

Knowledge-level changes. InstructReplay keeps assistant behavior close to M 0’s, and its MMLU score changes little, by − 1 . 35 points on average. The largest change, − 4 . 56 on CommonCorpus SmolLM3, does not come from output format, since the four options keep nearly all of the probability mass at the scored position, as they do for M 0. It also varies across training runs (Appendix G).

Retention weight. We chose λ = 0 . 25 among { 0 , 0 . 25 , 1 , 4 } on CommonCorpus with SmolLM3 and Qwen3, where it gave the highest S for both (Appendix E). Relative to no replay, which at λ = 0 is identical to Naive SFT, the selected replay

weight lowers F from 25.10 to 4.66 for SmolLM3 and from 20.33 to 1.40 for Qwen3. Larger weights trade acquisition for retention unevenly: at λ = 4, Qwen3 forgets slightly less but loses a quarter of its acquisition. Because λ was selected on these two settings, the four transfer settings, Phi-4-mini and all of PMC, are the fairer test of the recipe, and there InstructReplay leads the strongest baseline by 5.16 points or more in S .

Cost. InstructReplay doubles the sequences per step and replays about 3M response tokens per model, roughly an order of magnitude more than its domain tokens. Yet its PMC training takes at most 2.22 GPU-hours per model, against at least 4.83 for RAFT, and the buffer is generated once per model and shared across domains (Appendix E).

8 Conclusion

Fine-tuning small instruction-tuned models on new facts mainly damaged their generative behavior: generative benchmarks collapsed and responses shrank, while likelihood-scored MMLU barely moved. Replaying the initial model on general instructions largely prevented this damage at little cost to acquisition. In the controlled comparisons, assistant-turn replay gave lower average forgetting than BOS-conditioned and domain-answer replay, and self-generated answers gave lower average forgetting than the tested dataset answers. Soft and sampled targets performed similarly at the shared coefficient. For practitioners, about ten thousand general instructions answered once by the model being adapted are an inexpensive safeguard. Small MMLU declines under replay and larger models remain open.

Limitations

Evaluation. Domain acquisition relies on model judges without human calibration, judges of the same families also score RAFT’s training targets, and invalid-vote rates differ between methods. Some evaluation questions cue part of the answer. Publication dates alone cannot rule out that related facts appeared in pretraining.

Coverage of general ability. Our evaluation covers five general benchmarks that overlap in kind with the replay mixture; whether retention extends to abilities absent from the replay data, such as safety behavior or long-context use, remains untested. MMLU is scored by likelihood and is therefore insensitive to generation failures.

C Training and Baseline Implementation

C.1 Shared optimization

The training schedule is 300 optimizer steps with 32 effective domain sequences per step. The matched replay runs use eight devices, one sequence per device per microbatch and four accumulation steps. The released run-level matrix lists all 48 layouts. CommonCorpus SDFT uses four devices with accumulation eight; its other methods use eight with accumulation four. PMC SDFT uses the four-device layout, Self-Rewrite uses four or eight depending on the model, and the other methods use eight. Each microbatch contains one sequence per device, giving the same effective batch of 32. All paths use BF16. The maximum sequence length is 2,048 for SmolLM3 and Phi, 2,304 for every CommonCorpus Qwen path, and 2,304 for PMC Qwen except Naive SFT, DFT, and LoRA-SFT, which use 2,048. The sequence limits accommodate the respective encoded inputs. InstructReplay uses a constant 10_−5 learning rate with no warmup, response-only losses, and 32 additional retention sequences per step. Student and teacher share the exact initial model, tokenizer, and chat-template identity; the teacher stays frozen. C.2 Baseline targets and adaptation

LoRA-SFT uses rank 16, alpha 32, zero dropout, and no bias adaptation. It targets attention q/k/v/o

projections and feed-forward gate/up/down projections, with learning rate 10_−_4 . Naive SFT uses the original domain answers and full parameters. DFT uses detached target-token probabilities to weight negative log likelihood, using withinsequence means.

Self-Rewrite uses the exact M0 to rewrite domain answers with access to the gold answer, applies a generic-answer filter with fallback to the original, and trains full parameters on the resulting supervision. SDFT follows the practical forward-KL choice described in v2 of Shenfeld et al. (2026). It uses current-student responses and a demonstration-conditioned teacher updated once per optimizer step as θT ← 0 . 99 θT + 0 . 01 θ . Its loss is full-vocabulary teacher-to-student KL at temperature 1; generation is capped at 512 tokens within the total sequence limit. It has no separate general replay population.

RAFT follows Li et al. (2026b). It obtains an M0 rewrite, applies a cosine-similarity threshold of 0.8 using all-MiniLM-L6-v2, and uses Qwen3.8-27B for answer fusion on the retained path. Threejudge quality scoring stops fusion early at 0.8; if no candidate reaches that threshold, the highestscoring candidate is still selected. Online training combines supervision with answer-conditioned reference feedback on up to 64 student-generated tokens. KL uses the teacher’s top 512 tokens at temperature 1.5, normalized over that support.

C.3 Validation coverage

All 48 trained endpoints completed 300 steps, and each restored checkpoint passes a reload check: 39 by a paired save/reload comparison and nine by reproducing fixed-input logits and short greedy outputs across two independent restores.

All three instruction-replay buffers retain 9,600 prompts without rejection or replacement. The maximum Qwen replay length is 2,082 tokens, within its actual 2,304-token limit. A frozen-teacher forward is retained for diagnostics but excluded from the optimized hard loss. Its measured compute therefore includes work that a production hardreplay implementation could omit.

BOS replay uses the BOS-conditioned sampling of Marek et al. (2026) with the forward KL, coefficient and schedule of InstructReplay. The modelconfig BOS token initializes each sequence. Sampling uses temperature 1, top-p 1, and top-k 0, with EOS stopping disabled. Each row uses a fixed random state; the generated sequence is trimmed to that row’s instruction-response budget. The stored 9,600-row buffer is reused across the two domains for each model. This matches sequence counts and retained response positions per sequence; content, instruction context, padding and runtime depend on the replay input. The author implementation averages over valid tokens and generates replay in successive frozen-reference buffers. Our adaptation uses sequence means and a precomputed buffer, with per-row length matching and the common 1:1, 0.25, 300-step schedule. The authors’ instruction-model configuration instead uses a 4:1 domain/replay sequence ratio, five epochs, and a coefficient sweep over 1, 3, and 10.
