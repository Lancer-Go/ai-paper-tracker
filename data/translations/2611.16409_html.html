Experience-Handoff Reinforcement Learning for Recursive Self-Improvement

EXPERIENCE-HANDOFF REINFORCEMENT LEARNING FOR RECURSIVE SELF-IMPROVEMENT

Doyoung Kim1 , Byeongguk Jeon2 , Kailin Zhang1 , Sherry Yang1 1New York University 2KAIST AI {doyoungkim,sherryyang}@nyu.edu

ABSTRACT

It is common to train Large Language Models (LLMs) for agentic tasks with Reinforcement Learning (RL) from scalar rewards. However, this throws away the fine-grained information about the environment that the agent experienced during the trial (e.g., error logs). To utilize this rich information, recent works wrap the agent in a harness that refines its own inputs through reflection or compaction and hands past experience to future rollouts, but the construction of the handed-off artifacts (e.g., a playbook) is itself not learned. We want the best of both worlds: gradient updates from a scalar reward, while also utilizing environment information to hand off experience. As a concrete testbed, we fix the Agentic Context Engineering (ACE) harness and train one model to act, reflect, and curate tasklocal playbooks. Our method, ACE-RL, evaluates candidate experience-handoff artifacts by their downstream effect on future actor rollouts, and co-optimizes the role-specific behaviors (action generator, insight reflector, and playbook curator) under a fixed scaffold. Across agentic benchmarks, ACE-RL achieves higher held-out performance than baseline methods. The results suggest that agent training should optimize not only the actions in the current rollout, but also the model-written experience that future rollouts inherit.

1 INTRODUCTION

An agent that learns from its own trials needs experience handoff : what one attempt revealed about the environment has to reach the attempts that follow. There are two ways to hand off experience.

1

The first is through scalar rewards that compress the trial into a single number, which the learner absorbs into its weights. The second is through rich experience artifacts, such as reflections, notes, or playbooks, that preserve the context of the trial and are passed to future rollouts in the model’s input.

Reinforcement learning (RL) is the standard instance of the first way. During training, the agent hands off experience to its future self only through the policy update: each rollout is scored, the score becomes an advantage, and the gradient step shifts the policy toward rewarded behaviour (Williams, 1992; Sutton & Barto, 2018; Schulman et al., 2017). For language models, reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) reliably improves the actor across model families and scales (Shao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025b). However, outcome-only RL compresses a whole trial into one number, discarding the error logs, tool outputs, and discovered constraints that made the trial informative. The agent sees this environment feedback while it acts, but it cannot hand any of it off to future rollouts except through an abstract scalar, and RL offers no recipe for the reflection and note-writing behaviours that would carry it forward.

The second way is through harnesses : scaffolds that decide when the model acts, what it sees, and what it carries from one attempt to the next. Harnesses keep experience as text, such as verbal reflections (Shinn et al., 2023), skill libraries and reusable workflows (Wang et al., 2023; 2024), cheatsheets and playbooks (Suzgun et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2025b), evolved prompts (Agrawal et al., 2025), or archives of evaluated programs in test-time discovery loops (Novikov et al., 2025). With a capable frontier model inside, such scaffolds exhibit inference-time recursive self-improvement (RSI): a rollout fails, the model reflects on the trace, rewrites its own notes or context, and the next rollout, conditioned on those notes, does better. Throughout this paper, RSI refers only to this loop, in which a model improves its own future rollouts by rewriting the context it will inherit, under fixed weights and a fixed harness.1 However, the harness only preserves experience; the quality of what it hands off is bounded by the frozen model that writes it. The calls that reflect and edit are never trained for the effect of their text on future rollouts, and methods that optimize the harness itself (Agrawal et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026) change the prompts and code around the model rather than the model’s ability to write useful experience (Figure 2).

Each way therefore has what the other lacks: RL updates weights from a verifiable signal but hands off only a scalar, and harnesses hand off rich experience but never learn to write it. This paper asks:

Can a model learn, from task outcomes alone, both to act and to write the experience that its future self will inherit?

We refer to this kind of training as experience-handoff RL (EHRL). As a testbed we fix the ACE harness (Zhang et al., 2025b) and train one model for its three roles: the actor that solves the task, the reflector that extracts insights from a trajectory, and the curator that edits a task-local playbook for the next attempt. One property makes this training work: the final playbook is verifiable . Handing a candidate playbook to a fresh actor rollout and reading the task verifier scores it without a learned reward model, following the success behind RLVR. Our method, ACE-RL, pairs this reward with a comparison group for every role : each reflection is compared with other reflections of the same trajectory and each edit with other edits of the same insight, instead of every output in a chain sharing the chain’s final reward.

At equal training compute on Mastermind, MBPP, AppWorld, and MiniSWEBench, ACE-RL’s held-out reward in the harness is above that of baseline methods, including actor-only RL without the harness and end-to-end training through the harness with a learned critic. We also show ACE-RL performs better than the actor-only GRPO checkpoint in the same harness, which simulates the common practice of running a model inside a hand-built harness that it was never trained for. We also show that the lead of RSI@ K over GRPO’s pass@ K maintains up to K = 8, proposing that the helpfulness of the playbook adaptation extrapolates beyond the training cutoff ( K = 3). Finallly, we prove that training objective of ACE-RL actually makes helpful playbook rather than just adapting the actor component.

2 EXPERIENCE-HANDOFF REINFORCEMENT LEARNING

This section defines the harness and states the learning problem: the model is trained for the outcome of the harness it runs in, not only for the outcome of its actor.

2.1 A FIXED TASK-LOCAL HARNESS

The harness in this paper is a task-local variant of Agentic Context Engineering (ACE) (Zhang et al., 2025b). One model πθ serves three roles under fixed role instructions ι act , ι ref , ι cur. Let q be a task instance and P its current playbook , a short list of natural-language tips that is prepended to the actor’s input. One handoff iteration consists of

τ ∼ πθ ( · | ι act( q, P )) , (act) (1)
z ∼ πθ ( · | ι ref ( τ )) , (reflect) (2)
u ∼ πθ ( · | ι cur( P, z )) , P ′ = Apply( P, u ) . (curate) (3)

The actor trajectory τ interleaves generated actions with environment observations, and Rq ( τ ) ∈ [0 , 1] is its reward from the benchmark’s task verifier. The reflector reads the trajectory, including its reward and whatever the environment revealed (test failures, tool errors, termination reason) and writes an insight z . The curator reads the incumbent playbook and the insight and emits structured edit operations u (add, update, or remove a tip); Apply executes valid operations to produce a candidate playbook P′ . The playbook is local to q : a fresh environment episode is started for every actor call, and the only state carried across attempts on the same task is P . Prompts for all roles are listed in Appendix C.

Inference-time recursive self-improvement. At test time the harness runs K handoff iterations per task with frozen weights, and each iteration’s curator edit is applied to the playbook that the next attempt reads; no extra rollout scores the edit. We call this protocol RSI@K : a task counts as solved if any of the K iterations solves it. Section 4.2 specifies the protocol and its rollout accounting.

ACE-RL trains the actor, reflector, and curator together with a GRPO-style update for Eq. (5). It has two parts: a verifiable reward for every handoff output, obtained by executing the playbook it leads to (Section 3.1), and a sampling tree that gives each role its own comparison group within GRPO’s actor-rollout budget (Section 3.2). 3.2 A COMPARISON GROUP FOR EVERY ROLE

Group-relative advantages need several outputs sampled from the same input. Algorithm 1 and Figure 3 build these groups for task q with playbook P : N seed rollouts of ( q, P ); M reflections of one seed τj⋆ , j⋆ ∼ Unif[ N ]; C edits of one usable reflection zh , chosen uniformly, and one edit of each other usable reflection; and one fresh rollout per admissible edit (Eq. 6), which joins the seed rollouts in the actor group.

Each output i receives Ai = ( ri − µG ) / ( σG + ϵ ), where µG and σG are the reward mean and standard deviation of its role’s group G2 : actions against the other actor rollouts of q , reflections against the other reflections of τj⋆ , and edits against the other edits of ( P, zh ). A failed seed can therefore still yield a positively rewarded reflection. Groups with constant rewards are dropped, and the outputs of all three roles are optimized in one clipped update of the shared model (Eq. 24).

4 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP

We train Qwen3-0.6B in non-thinking mode (Qwen Team, 2025) on Mastermind and MBPP (Austin et al., 2021), and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 on AppWorld (Trivedi et al., 2024) and MiniSWEBench (Yang et al., 2024; Jimenez et al., 2023); Appendix E describes the tasks, splits, and reward design. Every actor episode has 20 turns (6 on MBPP) in training and evaluation. ACE-RL’s 8 actor rollouts per task are N = 4 seed rollouts and up to four rollouts that score candidate playbooks: M = 2 reflections of one seed, C = 3 edits of one reflection and one edit of the other (With N = 4, M = 2, and C = 3, a task yields at most four candidates, so the actor group holds at most eight rollouts, as in GRPO.). Each task’s playbook is reset every F = 3 encounters. Delayed-joint PPO samples one reflection and one edit per actor rollout. All methods share the harness code, verifiers, and optimizer settings, and Appendix D lists the remaining training details.

4.2 EVALUATION PROTOCOLS

Checkpoints are evaluated with frozen weights on held-out tasks at actor temperature 0 . 7. Vanilla evaluation draws eight independent actor episodes per task without any harness, and pass@ K is the expected best reward of K of them. Harnessed evaluation runs eight sequential visits: each is one actor episode under the current playbook, followed by one reflection and one curator edit that is applied for the next visit, with no separate candidate-scoring rollout. RSI@ K is the best reward among the first K visits; the reported test reward of both uses K = 3. All comparisons are at equal training compute, the cumulative update FLOPs of actor (plus critic for PPO and delayed joint PPO); Appendix F.1 gives the compute budget and the checkpoint chosen for each run. Each method is trained once, and error bars are one standard error over held-out tasks.

ERL, which hands its revised prompts off only through the weights, is below GRPO on all four benchmarks. PPO is also below GRPO on all four, and its test reward reaches zero on Mastermind and MiniSWEBench.

Training through the harness. Delayed-joint PPO trains the same three roles as ACE-RL, but every output of a chain receives the chain’s next-attempt reward, which a learned critic turns into advantages. This is the credit structure of recent systems that train a model whose own summaries become its future context (Li et al., 2026; Chan et al., 2026). It is below ACE-RL on all four benchmarks, and its test reward peaks early and then declines. On Mastermind its RSI@3 reaches 0 . 66 at 0 . 54 EFLOPs, against ACE-RL’s 0 . 74 at 0 . 46 EFLOPs, before its training reward falls below 0 . 05 by 0 . 73 EFLOPs. On MBPP it peaks at 0 . 53, against ACE-RL’s 0 . 99 at the budget. On AppWorld and MiniSWEBench it starts at 0 . 21 and 0 . 08 and falls to near zero (training reward in Appendix F.2). Its critic’s explained variance stays above 0 . 95 late in each run (Appendix F.3), so a poorly fit critic does not explain the decline. We cannot conclude that critic-based credit cannot train the handoff, but at least we can see that it’s more prone to training collapse.

We show that the practice may be effective on some benchmark, but ACE-RL still leads the performance on matched training compute. 5.4 WHERE DOES THE GAIN COME FROM?

We are also curious whether ACE-RL’s harness optimization itself is effective, or it’s just actor adaptation that has been improved. To find out, we repeat ACE-RL’s harnessed evaluation with every edit discarded, so each visit reads the empty playbook and the visits become independent attempts of the same actor (Figure 7). The playbook adaptation helps across benchmarks: within the training horizon, applying edits raises RSI@ K at K = 2 and K = 3 on all four, most clearly on AppWorld (0 . 105 ± 0 . 041 at K = 2) and MiniSWEBench (0 . 090 ± 0 . 040 at K = 3; ± 1 SEM). Beyond the horizon, the difference stays at or above zero at every K from 4 to 8 on MiniSWEBench, MBPP, and AppWorld, and on MiniSWEBench applying edits adds 0 . 04 to 0 . 07 at each of these K . 6 CONCLUSION

We formulated experience-handoff RL, which trains one model both to act and to write the experience that its future attempts inherit, and ACE-RL, which makes this practical within GRPO’s actor-rollout budget through a verifiable reward for the executed playbook and a comparison group for every role. At equal training compute on four benchmarks, ACE-RL reaches the highest held-out reward, above actor-only RL, ERL, and end-to-end training through the harness with a learned critic, and above a GRPO-trained model run in the same harness. The gain does not fade with more attempts: ACE-RL’s RSI@ K stays above GRPO’s pass@ K up to K = 8, beyond the three visits seen in training. Appendices A and H discuss related work and limitations.

A RELATED WORK

Harnesses and inference-time self-improvement. Reflexion, Voyager, ExpeL, Dynamic Cheatsheet, Agent Workflow Memory, and ACE let a frozen model improve later attempts by writing reflections, skills, or playbooks that its future calls consume (Shinn et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2024; Suzgun et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025b). This is the form of recursive self-improvement (RSI) we study. RSI more broadly also covers agents that rewrite their own code or scaffold (Schmidhuber, 2003; Zelikman et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025a), agents that iterate on programs or research code against an automated evaluator (Novikov et al., 2025; Karpathy, 2026), and models that update their own weights from self-generated data or test-time RL (Yuan et al., 2024; Zweiger et al., 2025; Yuksekgonul et al., 2026); see ang Gao et al. (2025) for a survey. We restrict RSI to rewriting context under fixed weights because that loop is an experience handoff between attempts. These systems assume a model that already writes

A complementary line optimizes the harness around a fixed model, by compiling prompts, evolving them reflectively, or searching over harness programs (Khattab et al., 2023; Agrawal et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2026). ACE-RL fixes the harness and trains the model.

Agentic RL with verifiable rewards. GRPO and its refinements train actors from group-relative advantages on verifiable outcomes (Shao et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025b; Liu et al., 2025), and multi-turn agentic RL extends this to tool use and long horizons (Wang et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2026b). Recent open agentic models report large-scale RL pipelines with learned or rubric-based rewards for intermediate agent behaviour (GLM-4.5 Team, 2025; Kimi Team, 2025). ACE-RL keeps the verifiable-reward ingredient and extends grouping from the actor to every model call in the harness, using downstream execution rather than a learned reward model to score intermediate text.

Training the model to a fixed harness. OpenClaw-RL trains a model on rollouts collected inside a production harness (Wang et al., 2026a). Composer 2, SUPO, CompactionRL, MEM1, and MemAgent train a policy whose own summaries or compactions become its future context, propagating the final reward through execution and summary segments with sequence-level advantages or a critic (Chan et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2025; Li et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025a). OpenClaw-RL has no model-written handoff to credit, and the compaction methods generate summaries sequentially within each rollout and credit them through the rollout’s outcome or a learned critic, without branching into alternative summaries from the same intermediate context. Our delayed-joint PPO baseline follows this structure (Section 4.1); Appendix B shows that, with an executed playbook as the reward, the chain-wise structure estimates the same gradient as ACE-RL, so same-input groups change the variance of the credit rather than its target.

Learning reflections and memories from downstream outcomes. Reflect–Retry–Reward rewards a reflection by the success of the retry it guides (Bensal et al., 2025); ERL adds internalization so that the guided behaviour is reproduced without the reflection (Shi et al., 2026); Memory-R1 trains memory operations by downstream answer correctness, freezing the answer agent during memory-manager training and training it separately (Yan et al., 2025); JERP learns experiential rules alongside an RL-trained actor with a frozen editor (Ye & Yu, 2026). Training-Free GRPO aggregates insights from several rollouts into a shared guidebook without weight updates (Cai et al., 2025). These are the closest precedents for verifiable credit on intermediate text. ACE-RL differs in training one policy for a two-stage handoff (reflect, then curate) with a persistent, editable artifact, and in constructing a comparison group for each stage.

Finer-grained grouping. GiGPO groups actions at repeated environment states and AT-GRPO forms agent- and turn-wise groups under shared inputs (Feng et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2025). ACE-RL applies the same principle to the dependency tree of a harness, and its sparse tree is a specific way to form such groups in a tree whose leaves must be evaluated by rollouts.

B.3 WHAT IT RESEMBLES

The chain-wise structure is the credit structure of end-to-end methods that train a model whose own summaries or compactions become its future context. Composer 2 and SUPO allow multiple successive summaries per chain and propagate the chain’s outcome to their generated tokens with group-relative advantages (Chan et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2025); delayed-joint ACE-RL uses analogous chain-level credit for a reflection and an edit, with the executed playbook in place of the continued episode. CompactionRL runs the same kind of chain with a learned critic (Li et al., 2026), which is our delayed-joint PPO baseline (Section 4.1). Delayed-joint ACE-RL thus sits between the two: it shares its chains with delayed-joint PPO and its reward with ACE-RL.

B.7 DISCUSSION

With the verifiable playbook reward held fixed, the choice between chain-wise comparison and per-role groups changes the variance of the handoff credit, not its target. In our runs at equal compute the two designs are close on Mastermind, and ACE-RL is ahead on MBPP, mostly at the first visit, on AppWorld at almost every checkpoint, and on MiniSWEBench within task-level noise; because the runs also differ in actor sampling, actor credit, and handoff prompts, these results do not isolate the effect of the grouping. Per-role groups remove the seed contribution to advantage variance under the assumptions of Appendix B.2, and ACE-RL reaches these results with half the actor rollouts of the chain-wise design. Measuring that variance directly, and establishing with additional training runs when it pays off, is left to future work.

Third, consecutive training encounters use changing policy checkpoints; identifying their score contributions requires an approximation that the policy changes slowly, or an explicit correction. Thus persistence motivates current actor training under the conditional interpretation, while it does not establish equality of the implemented update and the complete delayed gradient.

From the full tree to the sparse tree. The full tree above samples M reflections of every actor rollout, C edits of every reflection, and L evaluations of every candidate. Four choices yield the implemented scheme. • One reflector group. All N seed rollouts are kept for actor training, but only one uniformly chosen seed is reflected on. This preserves a same-input reflector group and removes the factor N from all downstream sampling.

  • One curator group. One uniformly chosen usable reflection receives C edits and every other usable reflection receives one. This preserves a same-input curator group and still supplies a downstream value for every reflection.

  • One evaluation per candidate. Replacing an average of L evaluations by one preserves the candidate’s conditional expected verifier reward, at higher conditional variance. Empirical standardization and the implementation choices discussed above qualify equivalence of the resulting updates.

  • Candidate rollouts as actor rollouts. The one rollout that scores each candidate is also added to the actor group, so the actor is trained on the N seed rollouts and on up to M − 1 + C candidate rollouts. This keeps the actor-rollout budget of GRPO, but the actor group then mixes inputs ( q, P ) and ( q, P′ ), so its normalization is no longer a same-input comparison.

D.2 PLAYBOOK SELECTION, RESET, AND THE TRAINING STEP

Training credit and memory selection are different decisions. The rewards above rank sampled outputs for learning; separately, the harness chooses which playbook task q carries into its next encounter by Eq. (8) (Section 3.2): among admissible candidates whose value is at least the seed’s reward, the one with the highest value is kept (ties broken uniformly), and otherwise P is kept. Rejecting an update does not erase its evaluated rewards; the corresponding outputs remain eligible for training subject to group filtering. Because edits accepted under early, weak parameters can persist and mislead later actors, we also reset each task’s playbook to P 0 every F training encounters of that task ( F = 3 in all main experiments). The reset bounds the age of retained experience and repeatedly exposes training to the empty-playbook condition that evaluation starts from. Appendix D.1 derives the sampling tree of Algorithm 1 as a reduction of the full tree: one evaluation per candidate preserves its expected verifier value while increasing estimation noise, and the finite-sample normalization need not preserve the full tree’s expected gradient.

D.3 IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS

Sampling and provenance. The main configuration samples N = 4 seed rollouts per task with the current playbook, selects one seed uniformly, samples M = 2 reflections of the seed, selects one usable reflection uniformly to receive C = 3 curator samples, and gives the other usable reflection one curator sample. With both reflections usable this yields 4 seed, 2 reflector, and 4 curator outputs and up to 4 candidate playbooks, each scored by one fresh actor episode on a newly initialized environment for the same task. These candidate episodes join the seed rollouts in the task’s actor group, so the actor trains on up to 8 rollouts per task, as in GRPO; the actor mini-batch of 64 rollouts also matches GRPO. Every candidate records its source task, seed trajectory, and reflection, so downstream values can be attributed to the outputs that produced them. Reflector and curator generations use the same sampling temperature (0 . 7) as the actor.

Reflector input. The benchmark adapter formats the seed trajectory for the reflector as a trajectory summary (outcome, tests passed or verifier result, number of turns, termination reason) followed by the full trajectory record (the actor’s messages interleaved with environment observations), truncated to a history budget of 20k tokens. The final environment reply, such as the last test failure or the grader’s verdict, is always kept when the history is clipped. On Mastermind, MBPP, and MiniSWEBench the reflector follows a three-step analysis (extract facts, explain the actor’s behaviour, and classify the case); on AppWorld it checks the requested result against the recorded attempt, identifies the earliest unresolved mismatch, and supplies an evidence-supported correction and a check before submission. Reflectors return a JSON object with outcome, reasoning, and key_insight. Prompts are listed in Appendix C.

Playbook and curator edits. A playbook is a list of lines [id] tip. The curator returns a JSON object with reasoning and a list of operations: ADD (new tip), UPDATE (rewrite the tip with a given bullet_id), or REMOVE (by bullet_id). Its prompt asks it to keep supported tips unless the evidence contradicts them. Operations are applied in order, duplicates are removed, and ids are renumbered. A curator output is admissible when it parses, contains at least one operation that applies to the current playbook, changes the playbook, and the resulting playbook is at most 2,048 tokens; otherwise it receives reward 0, is excluded from the set C ( z ) used to average its reflection’s reward, and produces no candidate. The initial playbook is empty on every benchmark.

Comparison groups and filtering. The seed and candidate rollouts of a task share the actor group; the two reflections share a group keyed by the seed trajectory; the three curator outputs of the selected reflection share a group keyed by that reflection; the other curator output is a singleton. MBPP. We use the full MBPP release (Austin et al., 2021), training on its 374-problem train split and evaluating on the first 100 problems of its test split. Each problem has three unit tests. The actor submits a program in a code block each turn; each assert runs in a subprocess with a 10-second timeout, and a turn’s reward is the fraction of asserts that pass. After a failed turn the environment shows up to three failing asserts with their error messages, and the episode ends when all tests pass or after six turns. The episode reward is the best turn reward, so held-out rewards are multiples of 1 / 3 per task. Because the displayed asserts are the ones that are scored, a harness can carry them forward; every method sees the same feedback. A first delayed-joint PPO run learned to end the test process with a zero exit code before a failing assertion could fail; the grader now counts a test only if a random marker printed after its assertion appears, and that run is excluded. All reported MBPP evaluations except those of delayed-joint ACE-RL and the delayed-joint PPO rerun predate this fix; ACE-RL and PPO evaluation trajectories at steps 170, 250, and 300 contain no exit calls.

We train on the 90 tasks of the train split and evaluate on the 57 tasks of the dev split.

G.1 WHAT THE TRAINED PLAYBOOKS CONTAIN

We inspected the playbooks that the fourth harnessed visit reads, after three handoffs, at the final ACE-RL checkpoints evaluated when this analysis was done: Mastermind and MBPP at step 300 and AppWorld at step 490. H LIMITATIONS

ACE-RL requires a harness whose handoff artifacts can be resampled and evaluated in isolation; agents that entangle acting, summarizing, and remembering in one generation fall back to chain-level credit, which our delayed-joint variant approximates. ACE-RL matches GRPO’s actor rollouts per task, but its reflector and curator calls still add generation to each step; our compute comparison (Appendix F.1) counts update FLOPs only. The playbook is task-local, and we do not study transfer of experience across tasks. Our evidence concerns models up to 4B parameters, one training run per method, and four benchmarks; the edits-discarded control on AppWorld is evaluated only beyond its compute budget, and the MBPP results use an environment whose feedback shows the scored assertions (Appendix E); whether the same recipe is needed, or sufficient, at frontier scale is open.
