DeliverBench: Benchmarking AI Agents on Delivering Realistic Multi-Stage Professional Projects

Abstract

LLM agents are judged by what they deliver, and in professional work what gets delivered is a project, not a task. A project pays off only if the whole package holds, and one weak part can limit the value of the rest. Yet existing benchmarks mostly test agents on one task at a time, leaving a gap between the capability we measure and the outcome that makes agents useful. To address this gap, we introduce DeliverBench , which tests whether agents can carry a project from brief to final delivery. Its 11 projects, spanning financial due diligence, legal contract drafting, and drug safety reporting, are reconstructed with 13 domain experts from real materials and chain 150 subtasks through 2–15 rounds of new materials and client feedback. Because no reference outputs are injected, agents inherit their own errors from one task to the next. Expert rubrics then score every checkpoint as well as the final package. Across seven model–harness systems, even the best system scores only 65.0 out of 100 on final delivery and passes 50.0% of subtasks at a 90-point threshold. The failures are often failures of carrying work forward: in one audit project, all seven systems addressed the review findings, but only one preserved traceability. Together, these results suggest that task-level capability does not simply compose into delivery, which also depends on keeping the work coherent as it evolves.

Evaluating this form of delegation requires examining how agents carry outputs through interdependent tasks toward a shared objective.

Existing benchmarks capture parts of this setting, but leave project delivery underexplored (Table 1). Professional-task benchmarks such as GDPval, APEX-Agents, and OfficeBench assess whether agents can complete bounded assignments [7, 13, 14]. DAYJOB also checks consistency across deliverables and requires every rubric criterion to be met for strict task success [2]. OSWorld 2.0 evaluates long-horizon computeruse workflows with dynamic task updates and checkpoint assessment [17], while AutomationBench checks business-workflow outcomes in application state [8]. Other benchmarks incorporate workplace environments, file dependencies, research projects, or end-to-end workflows [9, 12, 15, 20]. Interactive benchmarks also study policy-guided tool use [16] and proactive assistance across sessions [18]. Two requirements must be addressed together: project simulation, in which later tasks inherit and revise the agent’s own artifacts as new information arrives; and delivery evaluation, which assesses intermediate work as well as the joint requirements of the final package. Our central question is: How well can AI agents deliver multi-stage professional projects end to end?

We introduce DeliverBench , a benchmark that takes a multi-stage professional project as its evaluation unit. Domain experts provide original project materials, including source records, deliverables, feedback, and acceptance standards. The benchmark comprises 11 projects across seven domains, with 150 interdependent subtasks and 111 required final deliverables (Section 3.4). Agents work in a persistent workspace along a project timeline: task instructions, new materials, and client feedback arrive at prescribed stages. Later tasks use the agent’s earlier deliverables as working inputs, so data, assumptions, and conclusions must retain their meaning and evidential support as they move from analysis to decisions and formal documents. An upstream error can persist across several mutually consistent files, while a correctly identified risk can still be lost if it does not inform the next decision. Each project culminates in a multi-file package governed by shared delivery requirements. The challenge is therefore to maintain and update accumulated work across tasks, beyond completing any one lengthy assignment.

DeliverBench pairs process delivery evaluation with final project delivery evaluation. Expert-written rubrics assess checkpoint submissions against each subtask’s requirements and the information available at that point. The final package is evaluated through three complementary targets: artifact quality of individual files, crossfile consistency among them, and package acceptance against overall handoff requirements. Orthogonally, each criterion carries one primary quality-dimension label: correctness, completeness, consistency, professionalism, or usability. Evaluation targets specify what is evaluated; quality dimensions specify which aspect of quality is checked. Process and final scores remain separate, while preserved submissions and execution records support tracing how earlier work is reused, repaired, or left unresolved.

Our contributions are as follows.

  • A benchmark of real-world professional projects. We reconstruct projects supplied by domain experts as staged, interdependent workflows in a persistent workspace, testing whether agents can carry their own artifacts forward toward a shared multi-file delivery objective.

  • Evaluation beyond individual deliverables. We connect checkpoint assessment with final-package evaluation and diagnostics across five quality dimensions, capturing cross-file relationships and package acceptance requirements that isolated artifact scores leave untested.

  • Evidence of limited end-to-end delivery capability. Across seven model–harness systems, mean final-delivery scores range from 40.4 to 65.0, and subtask pass rates at the 90-point threshold range from 13.3% to 50.0%. 2 Related Work

Table 1 compares representative designs; we discuss three related lines of work below.

Professional and office tasks. GDPval [7] evaluates occupational work products, APEX-Agents [13] studies professional-service assignments in tool-rich worlds, and OfficeBench [14] covers cross-application workflows, including file creation and subsequent use. JobBench [4] organizes heterogeneous reference workspaces around occupational tasks and evaluates their outputs with fact-anchored rubric chains. Agents’ Last Exam (ALE) [11] evaluates expert-authored, long-horizon professional tasks involving real software and verifiable outputs. DAYJOB [2] pairs brief professional requests with large, heterogeneous workspaces in healthcare and finance; its criteria include consistency within a set of deliverables, and strict success requires meeting every criterion. DeliverBench studies how such delivery requirements are maintained across a multi-stage project, where outputs from one assignment become inputs to later assignments and new materials arrive during execution. This tests whether task-level competence produces artifacts that remain usable in subsequent work toward a shared business objective.

Workflow and project benchmarks. OSWorld 2.0 [17] evaluates end-to-end computer-use workflows involving realistic artifacts, stateful applications, and new information arriving during execution. AutomationBench [8] evaluates policy-guided business workflows through REST APIs, with dependent operations across simulated applications. WorkArena++ [1] composes executable enterprise subtasks with state dependencies, while TheAgentCompany [15] combines workplace tasks with intermediate milestones and feedback from simulated colleagues. PaperBench [9] evaluates complete research-replication repositories by executing them in a fresh environment, providing project-level functional acceptance. StartupBench [20] assesses final deliverables from long-horizon professional workflows; Workspace-Bench [12] models input-file dependencies and restores its workspace between independent tasks. In interactive settings, τ -bench [16] tests agents’ API use and policy adherence through conversations with simulated users, while π -Bench [18] evaluates proactive intent resolution and task completeness across sessions sharing a persistent workspace. DeliverBench makes project evolution explicit: instructions, new evidence, and client feedback arrive along a prescribed timeline, while later tasks inherit the agent’s own artifacts. This tests coordinated revision: new information must be incorporated into earlier work and the dependent deliverables, keeping the project consistent as it evolves.

Process and delivery evaluation. AgentBoard [5] measures incremental progress using state matching and subgoal completion. OSWorld 2.0 assesses workflow checkpoints against the final environment state, while AutomationBench reports strict task success alongside final-state completion diagnostics [8, 17]. TheAgentCompany provides checkpoint scores, and DevAI’s Agent-as-a-Judge evaluates development milestones linked by a requirement DAG [15, 21]. PaperBench measures partial replication through a final-submission rubric; Workspace-Bench and SQBench also use trajectory evidence for process or risk checks [9, 10, 12]. DAYJOB likewise allows its judge to inspect the final workspace and execution trajectory after the agent finishes [2]. These mechanisms need not preserve and separately score submissions along the original project timeline. DeliverBench preserves checkpoint submissions and evaluates them separately from the final package, whose assessment covers artifact quality, cross-file consistency, and package acceptance. This supports tracing whether earlier defects persist or are repaired, while cross-file and package checks reveal inconsistencies or omissions that individual-file scores cannot establish.

DeliverBench evaluates professional project delivery through intermediate submissions and a complete, consistent, and usable final package.

3.1 Professional Projects as the Evaluation Unit

A professional project delivery instance specifies a professional role, a shared business objective, initial materials, interrelated subtasks, and a final multi-file package. Each project combines three elements:

  • A shared delivery contract. Scope and acceptance requirements define what must be handed over. Individual files must meet professional standards and jointly satisfy package completeness, cross-file consistency, and the project objective. The package must give its intended recipients the information and conditions needed to review, act on, or continue the work.

  • Interdependent work in a persistent workspace. Subtasks have distinct instructions, and their outputs become working inputs to later tasks. Earlier results constrain later calculations, judgments, and formal documents; agents must check, revise, and integrate these artifacts to complete the downstream work. Checkpoints preserve submissions; stages organize delivery milestones and may contain several subtasks.

  • An evolving project timeline. New materials, client decisions, and scripted feedback arrive according to the project schedule, requiring updates to earlier work and affected deliverables.

Appendix B.1 illustrates these elements through P02, where due diligence informs financing terms, those terms enter a financial model, and the resulting analyses support lender coordination and final handoff.

Tasks share a persistent workspace along a project timeline. Execution starts from a project brief and initial files; instructions, additional data, and client feedback arrive at prescribed stages. Later tasks use the agent’s earlier deliverables as working inputs. No reference outputs are injected between tasks: all artifact handoffs contain the agent’s own work. Uncorrected errors therefore carry into downstream analyses, decisions, and the final package. The agent must detect and repair missing or defective upstream artifacts while completing the work that depends on them. When new evidence changes an input or invalidates an assumption, the agent must identify the calculations, conclusions, and handoff actions that depend on it and update their current versions. Checkpoint versions are preserved for evaluation, while the final package contains the versions intended for handoff.

4 Experiments and Analysis

4.1 Experimental Setup and Metrics

Agent systems and execution. We evaluate seven model–harness systems: GPT-5.6 Sol with Codex, Claude Opus 5 with Claude Code, Gemini 3.8 Flash with Gemini CLI, Qwen 3.8 Max with Qwen Code, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and GLM 5.2 with Claude Code, and Kimi K3 with Kimi CLI. Projects follow the continuous protocol in Section 3.3, using Harbor 0.20.0 and Docker with a four-hour execution limit per subtask. System versions and execution settings are specified in Appendix D.1.

Evaluation and reporting. GPT-5.6 Luna judges rubric criteria; deterministic scoring produces separate process and final-delivery scores (Equation 1). We evaluate all 11 projects, yielding 77 system–project outcomes and 150 checkpoint submissions per system. Process and final-delivery scores are averaged equally across projects; the process score first averages subtasks within each project. We additionally report the fraction of checkpoint submissions scoring at least 90, pooling all 150 subtasks per system with equal weight. This thresholded subtask pass rate measures the frequency of high-scoring intermediate submissions, rather than final-package acceptance. Each system–project pair contributes one reported outcome; Appendix D.2 specifies failure and recovery handling. Appendix C specifies scoring, rubric reuse, project means, and subtask pass rates.

4.2 Main Results and Analysis

Figure 5 summarizes delivery performance, process–delivery gaps, resource use, and quality profiles across seven agent systems and 11 projects. Appendix E.1 provides the complete final-delivery and process-score matrices.

Current agents struggle with reliable end-to-end project delivery. Final-delivery scores range from 40.4 to 65.0 out of 100, with Claude Opus 5 scoring 65.0 and GPT-5.6 Sol scoring 63.7 (Figure 5a). At the 90-point threshold, subtask pass rates range from 13.3% to 50.0%: Claude passes 75 of 150 subtasks, GPT passes 54 (36.0%), and each remaining system passes at most 26.7%. Even the strongest observed pass rate covers only half of the subtasks, indicating that agents do not consistently produce intermediate deliverables that meet this high-quality threshold. Project delivery requires carrying these intermediate outputs through subsequent tasks and integrating them into a coherent final package. The low subtask pass rates and limited final-delivery scores together show that current systems fall short of sustaining high-quality delivery from intermediate work through final handoff.

Local task scores do not establish final-package readiness. Every system scores higher on process evaluation than on final delivery, with delivery gaps of 11.3–18.5 points (Figure 5b). For example, Claude scores 79.8 on intermediate work and 65.0 on final delivery, while GLM scores 63.0 and 44.5, respectively. This pattern also holds beyond system averages: 69 of 77 system–project outcomes lie below the equal-score line in Figure 5c. Artifact quality checks its evidential basis, cross-file consistency checks agreement across the resulting documents, and package acceptance checks whether the combined deliverables meet the recipient’s requirements. These averages do not locate the failure within an individual project. For example, Claude’s P05 package scores 88.8 on artifact quality and 100.0 on cross-file consistency, but only 60.0 on package acceptance: it leaves authorization contingencies, evidence-closure conditions, and the review-to-issuance sequence insufficiently specified for the recipient. In P03, all seven systems satisfy the four criteria directly addressing the incoming review issues, yet only four satisfy the

9

associated propagation check and only one satisfies the associated cross-file traceability check. Recording feedback, updating all affected artifacts, and handing over an actionable package are therefore distinct demands.

Content coverage exceeds operational quality. Completeness is the highest-scoring quality dimension for every system (68.0–84.9; mean 75.8), while usability averages 54.4, professionalism 57.1, correctness 58.7, and consistency 64.1 (Figure 5e). Covering requested content therefore does not ensure accurate, professionally presented, and usable outputs. These five quality dimensions cut across the three final-delivery targets.

Greater resource use does not consistently yield better delivery. Following joint evaluation of agent performance and cost [3], Figure 5d reports mean project time of 2.05–10.51 hours and token usage of 26.0M–184.5M. GPT and Claude score 63.7 and 65.0, respectively, but use 2.05 versus 10.51 hours and 36.4M versus 124.1M tokens per project; Appendix D.3 details accounting for the evaluated model–harness systems.

4.4 Evaluation Reliability

Comparisons with human judgments provide an empirical check on model-based evaluation [19]. We assess evaluator reliability by comparing expert and evaluator judgments on final deliverables produced by GPT-5.6 Sol–Codex for three projects: P01, P05, and P10. Across 478 rubric decisions, the judgments agree on 446 (93.31%); agreement is 93.04% for artifact quality, 95.00% for cross-file consistency, and 92.45% for package acceptance. Agreement is the fraction of matching binary judgments, pooled across criteria without deliveryscore weights. This validation covers final-delivery judgments for one agent system, including source-task criteria applied to its final artifacts.

5 Conclusion and Limitations

DeliverBench evaluates agents on multi-stage professional projects reconstructed with domain experts. Later tasks directly reuse the agent’s earlier deliverables, making the quality of upstream work consequential for downstream analysis and final handoff. It assesses intermediate submissions and final packages as agents incorporate new information and revise earlier artifacts in a persistent workspace.

Across seven model–harness systems, the highest mean final-delivery score is approximately 65 out of 100, and the highest subtask pass rate is 50% at the 90-point threshold. Every system has a lower mean final-delivery score than mean process score, and 69 of 77 system–project outcomes exhibit this gap. Case studies reveal conflicting conclusions, unapplied corrections, stale handoff states, and superseded decisions; they also show the value of repairing missing upstream artifacts. Reliable delivery requires tracing the effects of new evidence, translating analytical findings into current decisions, and repairing upstream artifacts together with the downstream work that uses them. Evaluating final packages alongside intermediate submissions makes these relationships and acceptance requirements explicit. More reliable project delivery could broaden enterprise delegation and support one-person companies, where an individual sets business goals and oversees agents through final handoff.

Our study has several limitations. Coverage remains limited to eleven projects across seven domains. Prescribed information releases and feedback support reproducible evaluation but do not fully capture open-ended client communication or live business decisions. Future work can broaden project coverage, introduce richer interaction, and extend expert validation to assess delivery capability more comprehensively in professional settings.

A Benchmark Comparison Details

Table 1 summarizes support specified in published protocols: a checkmark denotes explicit support, a triangle limited or related support, and a cross support not specified. These marks describe benchmark design rather than model performance or the prevalence of a feature across instances.

Evaluation unit. The unit is the scope of one evaluated instance, rather than its surrounding environment, individual tool actions, or rubric criteria. A project world can contain independent professional tasks, as in APEX-Agents [13, Sections 3.1–3.3]. Multiple output files alone do not change a task into a staged project. The labels describe scope rather than impose a common duration or file count.

Evolving inputs/feedback. Full support requires new task-relevant materials, requirements, or substantive collaborator feedback during execution. Scheduled releases and simulated-manager feedback qualify; ordinary tool observations, errors, continuation prompts, and retrieval of pre-existing files do not. TheAgentCompany incorporates simulated-manager feedback [15, Section 3 and Appendix C]; DeliverBench releases materials and client feedback across stages (Section 3.3).

Long-horizon tasks. Support follows an explicit benchmark objective or documented substantial compositional execution, without a common numerical step threshold. SQBench receives partial support because it mixes atomic capabilities with composite and domain workflows [10, Sections 3.2–3.4].

Explicit subtasks and artifact handoff. Full subtask support requires instantiated component tasks within a workflow; steps, checkpoints, or hierarchical requirements within one assignment receive partial support. Full artifact-handoff support requires later, separately specified tasks to consume or revise the same agent’s earlier outputs. Dependencies within one workflow, such as OfficeBench’s spreadsheet-to-email sequence, receive partial support [14]. Dependencies among supplied inputs alone do not qualify. Workspace-Bench restores the workspace between independent tasks [12, Section 4.5].

Intermediate evaluation. Full support requires checks or scores for defined intermediate subtasks or milestones, whether evaluated online or retrospectively. WorkArena++ and TheAgentCompany provide such checks [1, 15]. Partial support covers partial-progress assessment without defined subtask or milestone checks, or general retrospective process checks. Retrospective checkpoint scoring does not itself establish separately preserved stage submissions.

Project delivery. Full support requires evaluating an integrated multi-file submission against a shared project outcome, regardless of whether the benchmark calls the instance a task or project. DAYJOB’s cross-deliverable consistency checks qualify [2, Section 3.2], as does full PaperBench’s repository reproduction protocol [9, Sections 2.1–2.2]. This column does not additionally require staged inputs. Task-level output checks receive partial support where a project-package acceptance protocol is unspecified; WorkArena++ and AutomationBench instead validate application task state.

OSWorld 2.0 and AutomationBench. The OSWorld 2.0 row follows its published 108-task protocol. Dynamic messages support evolving inputs, and task-specific workflow checkpoints support intermediate evaluation, although scored from final state. Its reimbursement case evaluates a claim together with three generated supporting documents against shared requirements, qualifying for project delivery. Within-workflow artifact reuse and dependent steps support partial handoff and subtask structure; they are not separately specified project tasks [17, Sections 2.1–2.2 and Appendix H.1.1]. AutomationBench’s dependent API operations, including creating an Asana record and subsequently updating it, support partial handoff and subtask structure, and its compositional business workflows support the long-horizon column. Its single initial instruction and pre-seeded records do not establish evolving inputs. Acceptance checks application state rather

than a multi-file project package. Final-state assertion diagnostics support limited intermediate evaluation; official task success requires all assertions to pass [8, Sections 2–4 and 11].

Boundary cases and evidence. DAYJOB’s post-attempt trajectory inspection and partial-credit reporting support partial intermediate evaluation, while its initial workspace and non-interactive attempt do not establish evolving inputs or separate artifact handoffs [2, Sections 3.1–3.2 and 4.1–4.4]. C.2 Criterion Scope, Scoring, and Reuse

Criteria specify a task requirement, target artifacts, weight, and judgment evidence. Checkpoint judgments use the submitted stage versions and information conditions; final judgments use the handoff versions and requirements in effect at completion. Artifact-quality assessment reuses applicable source-task criteria while preserving their identities. A criterion or source group contributes once to the final aggregate, even when it covers several files. Previous verdicts are reused only when the relevant artifacts, requirements, and evidence context are unchanged; otherwise the criteria are judged again. Figure 4 counts each unique criterion once.

D.2 Run Provenance and Failure Handling

The results comprise 77 selected system–project outcomes and 1,050 checkpoint evaluations, with one reported outcome per pair. Job configurations request one attempt and disable automatic trial retries. Infrastructure recovery can resume preserved checkpoints or create additional attempt records; these are not independent repetitions. If judging or storage fails after a successful agent submission, recovery preserves that submission instead of regenerating it. Historical scores from different configurations are not pooled without reassessment.

Missing deliverables in completed submissions are evaluated by the applicable criteria and gates. Pending runs, infrastructure interruptions, and missing judgments retain explicit status; completed evaluations with triggered fatal conditions retain their zero final scores. For the evaluated GLM system, Claude Code’s native PDF and image payloads are unsupported: a compatibility guard returns recoverable tool errors while keeping local files and extraction tools available. This interface adaptation is recorded with the run provenance.
