A Practical Investigation of Training-free
Relaxed Speculative Decoding

Guoxuan Xia, Luka Ribar, g.xia21@imperial.ac.uk
Paul Balanca paulb@graphcore.ai
Work done at Graphcore
Abstract

Speculative decoding accelerates sampling from an autoregressive LLM by using a faster auxiliary model to draft tokens which are then verified in parallel by the LLM. Standard speculative decoding is lossless: its rejection and resampling steps exactly preserve the LLM’s sampling distribution. Recent work argues that relaxing this strict guarantee can yield further speed-ups, controlled capability-speed trade-offs, or even capability gains. We practically investigate training-free relaxed speculative decoding techniques, unify existing approaches within a shared framework, benchmark them on contemporary settings, and distil takeaways and empirical findings for practitioners. Important takeaways include: relaxation can require considerable capability evaluation unlike lossless speculative decoding, and many relaxed approaches rely on a drafter that is a good language model, making them unsuited for lightweight dedicated multi-token-prediction drafters. Code is available at: https://github.com/graphcore-research/relaxed-spec-dec-repro.

1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) dominate modern applications of machine learning (sajadieh2026aiindex). Language modelling as a task is itself in turn dominated by autoregressive (AR) models (transformer; gpt3), i.e. models that predict one token after another in series given previous tokens. Such AR models represent the frontier of capability, enabling transformative outcomes in programming, scientific research, and many other applications (sajadieh2026aiindex). A core issue, however, is the inherent slowness of generation that arises from the requirement to sequentially produce tokens, especially in an era of abundant parallel compute resources (you2026globalaicomputingcapacity). Applications often require lower latencies in order to satisfy user requirements (anthropic_haiku45_2025; openai_codex_spark_2026) – intuitively, snappier LLM responses enable greater human productivity.

One solution to this problem is speculative decoding (spec-dec) (spec-dec), which is motivated by the idea that for text generation, weaker but faster models may still be able to “speculate” decent short-range spans of text. Given an AR LLM, spec-dec employs a low-latency auxiliary model to “draft” tokens quickly, which are then “verified” in parallel using the LLM. This better utilises parallel compute and reduces overall latency. A key feature of this algorithm is that “bad” draft tokens are stochastically rejected and resampled during verification in such a way that strictly preserves the LLM’s output distribution, giving theoretically lossless acceleration. Speculative decoding is seeing increasing adoption across a wide range of models, such as DeepSeek and Qwen (liu2024deepseekv3technicalreport; qwen35), and inference frameworks such as vLLM and SGLang (kwon2023efficient; zheng2024sglang; schmittulms2025speculators).

Recently, a new body of research has argued that the strict distribution preservation of speculative decoding is disadvantageous; generally, it proposes to relax this requirement in favour of sampling from an alternative distribution to the original LLM. There are a number of motivations for doing this, for example, to enable the option of trading off task capability for speed-up (holsman-etal-2025-fuzzy), increasing speed-up by accepting semantically valid tokens that strict speculative decoding would reject (bachmann2025judge), or even improving task capability (yuan-etal-2024-spec-cont-dec). In this work, we perform a practical investigation of such relaxed speculative decoding approaches (in particular, those that are training-free). We aim to better inform practitioners and researchers about the practical utility of such approaches, given the variety of methods and experimental settings in recent literature. To this end, we present the following key contributions:

  1. 1.

    We provide a primer on strict speculative decoding (strict spec-dec), presenting the algorithm and explaining why and when it produces latency speed-ups. We also discuss how speed-up is measured and modelled, as well as how inference infrastructure (i.e. the hardware-software stack) can affect realisable speed-up.

  2. 2.

    We build a taxonomy over the literature of relaxed speculative decoding (relaxed spec-dec), organising diverse methods and narratives under a unified framework. The aim is for the reader to better understand not only how existing approaches are concretely implemented, but also their motivating scenarios, and how they relate to each other.

  3. 3.

    We benchmark a range of relaxed speculative decoding approaches on modern drafter-verifier pairs and reasoning benchmarks over different inference-time parameter settings. This allows us to not only fairly compare approaches amongst themselves, but also against their respective paper narratives. This is important as the literature suffers generally from narrow and disparate experimental settings that preclude easy comparison.

  4. 4.

    In relation to the problem setting of (relaxed) spec-dec, we extract practical Takeaways for the reader. Based on our experimental investigation of relaxed spec-dec methods, we also present practical empirical Findings.

2 Preliminaries

We provide a technical introduction to strict speculative decoding and then introduce a unified framework for relaxed speculative decoding. A glossary of notation can be found in Appendix˜A for the benefit of the reader.

2.1 Speculative Decoding (strict spec-dec)

As previously mentioned, many applications of machine learning are dominated by autoregressive (AR) LLMs that necessarily need to generate one token after the other in sequence, and this is an issue as lower generation latency is important for many applications. At the same time, AR LLM decoding is generally memory bound, that is to say, latency is dominated by repeatedly moving model weights and KV-cache activations, leaving available parallel compute underutilised (ma2026infhard). The consequence of this spare compute capacity is that increasing parallelism over tokens (e.g. via batching) has a sublinear, often very small, impact on latency.

Following from the above, speculative decoding (spec-dec) is an algorithm that aims to better utilise available compute resources via parallelisation in order to reduce generation latency. Importantly, it provably samples from the same distribution as the LLM-to-be-accelerated, i.e. it is lossless acceleration. Algorithm˜1 describes the approach: a fast auxiliary drafter model qq rapidly proposes an NdraftN_{\text{draft}}-long segment, which is then verified in parallel by the LLM-to-be-accelerated pp (the verifier). Each draft token x∈{0,…,V−1}x\in\{0,\dots,V-1\}, where VV is the vocab size, is stochastically accepted/rejected based on Bernoulli random variable a∼Ber⁡(min⁡{1,p​(x)/q​(x)})a\sim\operatorname{Ber}\!\left(\min\!\left\{1,p(x)/q(x)\right\}\right). At the first rejection (a=0a=0), the rejected token is resampled from the residual distribution norm⁡(max⁡{0,p​(⋅)−q​(⋅)})\operatorname{norm}\!\left(\max\{0,p(\cdot)-q(\cdot)\}\right), where norm\operatorname{norm} simply normalises to a valid probability distribution, recovering the distribution of pp losslessly. A detail that is sometimes overlooked is that the verifier pp also produces next-token logits for the final drafter token. If all draft tokens are accepted, then a bonus token is sampled from these logits. In fact, amongst algorithms that sample from pp by drafting a token from qq, and then randomly rejecting and resampling, the above achieves the lowest possible expected rejection rate (spec-dec-theory).

Modelling and understanding speed-up.

Intuitively, we expect speed-ups to be large when 1) the cost of the auxiliary drafter is much lower than the cost of verification, 2) the cost of parallel verification is not much more than a single AR decoding step, and 3) long draft segments are accepted. We will formalise this intuition in this section. The speed-up SS achieved by speculative decoding can be quantified as,

S=spec-dec token throughputAR token throughput=time for AR to generate l¯accept+1 tokenstime to draft and verify Ndraft tokens≈(l¯accept+1)​CARNdraft​Cdrafter+Cverify,S=\frac{\text{{spec-dec} token throughput}}{\text{{AR} token throughput}}=\frac{\text{time for {AR} to generate $\bar{l}_{\text{accept}}+1$ tokens}}{\text{time to draft and verify $N_{\text{draft}}$ tokens}}\approx\frac{(\bar{l}_{\text{accept}}+1)C_{\text{AR}}}{N_{\text{draft}}C_{\text{drafter}}+C_{\text{verify}}}, (1)

where NdraftN_{\text{draft}} is the draft length, l¯accept\bar{l}_{\text{accept}} is the average number of accepted draft tokens at NdraftN_{\text{draft}}, CARC_{\text{AR}} is the time for one AR decoding step of the main LLM pp, CdrafterC_{\text{drafter}} is the time for one decoding step of the auxiliary drafter model qq,111We note that drafting doesn’t have to be autoregressive, and some recent work proposes one-step parallel drafting (an2026pard; chen2026dflashblockdiffusionflash). Modelling such approaches would require a simple adjustment to the denominator of Eq. 1 to Cdrafter+CverifyC_{\text{drafter}}+C_{\text{verify}}. In this work, we will focus on autoregressive drafters as they represent the majority of existing drafters for speculative decoding. and CverifyC_{\text{verify}} is the time for one parallel verification step. Eq.˜1 shows that for greater speed-up, verification cost should be minimised, i.e. close to the cost of a single original AR step, Cverify≈CARC_{\text{verify}}\approx C_{\text{AR}}. This typically happens, as discussed above, when AR generation is memory bound so additional parallel compute has a small effect on inference latency. If the compute burden for each verification step is increased by higher token parallelism, then CverifyC_{\text{verify}} may become meaningfully larger than CARC_{\text{AR}}, reducing speed-up. Empirical results support this, with investigations showing that increasing batch size can reduce speed-ups (singh2025acceleratingllminference; liu2026speculativedecodingperformanceillusion). In the later analysis of this work, we will make the common assumption Cverify≈CARC_{\text{verify}}\approx C_{\text{AR}}, allowing us to define the relative cost crel=Cdrafter/CARc_{\text{rel}}=C_{\text{drafter}}/C_{\text{AR}} giving a simplified speed-up model,

S≈l¯accept+11+Ndraft​crel.S\approx\frac{\bar{l}_{\text{accept}}+1}{1+N_{\text{draft}}c_{\text{rel}}}. (2)

Average accepted draft length l¯accept\bar{l}_{\text{accept}} is intuitively higher when the drafter distribution is better aligned (over the draft length) with the verifier pp. In fact, the per-token acceptance probability (on average over the drafter distribution),

Pr⁡(accept draft token)=𝔼​[a]=1−TV​(q,p)=1−12​∑x|p​(x)−q​(x)|,\Pr(\text{accept draft token})=\mathbb{E}[a]=1-\text{TV}(q,p)=1-\tfrac{1}{2}\sum\nolimits_{x}|p(x)-q(x)|, (3)

is related to the total-variation distance between qq and pp (spec-dec-theory). Considering both Eqs.˜1 and 2 we can see that we want to make design choices that reduce the cost of the drafter CdrafterC_{\text{drafter}} and increase the average accepted length l¯accept\bar{l}_{\text{accept}}. The draft length NdraftN_{\text{draft}} on the other hand is an inference-time hyperparameter, and is subsequently optimised for maximum speed-up after other design choices are selected. It is important to note that the above factors interact, complicating the maximisation of speed-up. A more lightweight drafter could reduce both CdrafterC_{\text{drafter}} and l¯accept\bar{l}_{\text{accept}} and may only achieve greater final speed-up SS after NdraftN_{\text{draft}} is re-optimised. Simply decreasing drafter cost without changing its distribution qq, e.g. via kernel or hardware optimisations, may change the optimal value of NdraftN_{\text{draft}}. This is shown on the left of Fig.˜2. Suppose we have a drafter that achieves l¯accept=2.5\bar{l}_{\text{accept}}=2.5 when Ndraft=3N_{\text{draft}}=3 and l¯accept=5\bar{l}_{\text{accept}}=5 when Ndraft=8N_{\text{draft}}=8; if we model speed-up according to Eq.˜2 as crelc_{\text{rel}} is reduced, the better choice of draft length flips, since it becomes cheaper to draft more tokens, i.e. longer NdraftN_{\text{draft}} is “unlocked”. Moreover, speed-up is sensitive to crelc_{\text{rel}}, increasingly so as crelc_{\text{rel}} is reduced. Suppose we can also improve l¯accept\bar{l}_{\text{accept}} via better drafter alignment; we see that the (multiplicative) effect is highest when crelc_{\text{rel}} is low and NdraftN_{\text{draft}} is high. Moreover, unlocking a longer NdraftN_{\text{draft}} can give greater headroom for l¯accept\bar{l}_{\text{accept}} to improve. Thus, a practictioner may choose to focus on reducing crelc_{\text{rel}} first, and then consider improving l¯accept\bar{l}_{\text{accept}}.

We note the existing research often targets a single aspect (e.g. draft acceptance or drafter cost) and then compares speed-up under a limited setting. For example, an increase in l¯accept\bar{l}_{\text{accept}} may be measured in a paper for a specific NdraftN_{\text{draft}}, however, real-world CdrafterC_{\text{drafter}} may lead a practitioner to choose a lower optimal NdraftN_{\text{draft}} where real speed-up improvements are much lower. “Real-world” speed-ups may also be measured in settings where neither crelc_{\text{rel}} nor NdraftN_{\text{draft}} are well optimised, failing to provide readers with a clear idea of how an approach may translate to realisable improvements. For example, liu2026speculativedecodingperformanceillusion; hao2026cactus use vLLM to measure speed-up, however, we find that for separate draft models, vLLM has crelc_{\text{rel}} much higher than what could be ideally achieved (see Tab.˜1). In this paper, we choose to leverage Eq.˜2 to illustrate a more holistic view of speed-up for the reader: for different methods, we measure l¯accept\bar{l}_{\text{accept}} at different draft lengths NdraftN_{\text{draft}} and estimate speed-ups for different relative drafter costs crelc_{\text{rel}}. As previously discussed, and shown in the left of Fig.˜2, a speed-up model like Eq.˜2 allows a practitioner to understand the impact on speed-up of different optimisations, allowing them to make more informed improvements.

2.2 Relaxing Speculative Decoding (relaxed spec-dec)

In this paper, we are interested in investigating a recent area of research which argues that the exact preservation of the verifier distribution pp is too “strict” and that by “relaxing” this requirement, advantages can be obtained over strict spec-dec. We narrow our scope to training-free approaches that apply a per-token relaxation away from pp/strict spec-dec, to keep comparisons manageable. Such approaches can be applied directly to existing spec-dec setups without the overhead of additional training and avoid the associated risk of distribution drift from the training data. We start by introducing a framework that unifies existing approaches and then discuss some practical caveats of deployment.

Our framework is laid out in Algorithm˜2. We introduce relaxed target distributions π\pi that replace pp at the rejection, residual sampling, and bonus sampling steps in the original strict spec-dec algorithm (πrej,πres,πbonus\pi^{\text{rej}},\pi^{\text{res}},\pi^{\text{bonus}}). The relaxed target distributions π​[q,p,x,α]​(⋅)\pi[q,p,x,\alpha](\cdot) depend on a relaxation parameter α\alpha and can potentially depend on the drafter and verifier distributions p,qp,q as well as the drafted token xx. Given the diverse presentation in the literature, our framework allows us to cleanly describe and compare various existing approaches under a unified setting.

We note that “relaxing” the target distribution is typically motivated by the idea of trading off task capability for generative speed by accepting more draft tokens than strict spec-dec (narasimhan2025faster; holsman-etal-2025-fuzzy) (we will discuss paper-specific motivations and narratives later). This is illustrated in the right of Fig.˜2. Practically, deployment is either capability constrained or speed constrained, that is to say one must achieve a target capability or speed. Which type of constraint then determines what must be evaluated in order to tune the relaxation parameter α\alpha for deployment. Therefore, a practitioner should consider the cost of evaluation when deciding whether to choose to deploy relaxed speculative decoding. For example, it can be easy to measure latencies on the fly for a speed-constrained edge application and accordingly adjust α\alpha, however, the cost of evaluating the capability of a frontier generalist model across all intended deployment scenarios may be considerable (liang2023helm; huggingface2026evalscompute; openai2026swebenchverified). Crucially, strict spec-dec doesn’t require any capability re-evaluation (in theory) as it preserves the distribution of original model pp.222There still may be numerical effects that lead to deviations, so small-scale sanity checks may still be necessary.

One important detail that many existing works neglect is that deviating from pp via relaxation may change the average generated response length vs AR or strict spec-dec, which may affect overall generation time and thus speed-up. Thus for relaxed speculative decoding speed-up should be estimated using,

Srelax≈L¯ARL¯relax​(l¯accept+1)​CARNdraft​Cdrafter+Cverify≈L¯ARL¯relax​l¯accept+11+Ndraft​crel,S_{\text{relax}}\approx\frac{\bar{L}_{\text{AR}}}{\bar{L}_{\text{relax}}}\frac{(\bar{l}_{\text{accept}}+1)C_{\text{AR}}}{N_{\text{draft}}C_{\text{drafter}}+C_{\text{verify}}}\approx\frac{\bar{L}_{\text{AR}}}{\bar{L}_{\text{relax}}}\frac{\bar{l}_{\text{accept}}+1}{1+N_{\text{draft}}c_{\text{rel}}}, (4)

where L¯AR,L¯relax\bar{L}_{\text{AR}},\bar{L}_{\text{relax}} are the average generation lengths for AR and relaxed spec-dec. Intuitively, if a relaxed distribution π\pi tends to generate longer/shorter responses than pp then the effective speed-up will decrease/increase.

3 Related Work

We discuss literature that is relevant to accelerating speculative decoding, and thus may be of interest to the reader, but exists outside of the scope of this paper’s investigation.

Learned relaxed speculative decoding.

A number of works, motivated by the idea that strict spec-dec may reject draft tokens that are still semantically correct, suggest to learn a module that decides dynamically to accept/reject draft tokens. Judge Decoding (bachmann2025judge), inspired by LLM-as-a-Judge, proposes to train an auxiliary classifier on manually annotated data for this purpose, whilst follow-up AutoJudge (garipov2026autojudge) removes manual annotation by automatically verifying downstream correctness. DIVERSED (wang2026diversed) uses reinforcement learning based on task rewards to learn a module that dynamically adjusts a relaxed target distribution. As mentioned before, these are out of scope for our investigation due to their additional complexity and reliance on an auxiliary training data distribution.

Accelerating strict spec-dec.

Since it was originally introduced by spec-dec, there have been a varied range of approaches proposed to further accelerate strict spec-dec. SpecInfer (specinfer) and SEQUOIA (SEQUOIA) use tree-based drafting to generate multiple drafts and increase acceptance. REST (he-etal-2024-rest) uses text retrieval to propose drafts at a low cost, rather than using a neural network for the drafter. SpecDec++ (huang2025specdecpp) proposes to train an additional module that adaptively varies the draft length during generation. Block Verification (sun2025blockverification) perform rejection jointly on blocks of draft tokens, rather than token by token, increasing draft acceptance. Speculative Speculative Decoding (kumar2026specspec) parallelises drafting and verification by having the drafter precompute continuations for likely verifier outcomes whilst verification is ongoing, thereby hiding drafter latency if the verification outcome is successfully pre-empted. DistillSpec (zhou2024distillspec) and LK Losses (samarin2026lklosses) train the drafter to better align with the verifier distribution, increasing draft token acceptance rates.

Dedicated drafter architectures.

There has been strong trend in the community towards researching lightweight dedicated drafter modules that can rapidly propose short drafts. Such approaches aim to minimise drafter cost whilst maintaining decent draft acceptance, leading to strong practical speed-up (see Fig.˜2 left). Medusa (cai2024medusa) uses multiple prediction heads for drafting, whilst the EAGLE series (li2024eagle; li2026eagle3) propose to use a shallow auxiliary autoregressive transformer. Recently PARD (an2026pard), D-Flash (chen2026dflashblockdiffusionflash) and DSpark (cheng2026dspark) explore further acceleration via parallel/semi-autoregressive draft token generation. These are now generally referred to as multi-token-prediction (MTP) modules and are seeing increasing real-world adoption, frequently appearing in LLM releases such as DeepSeek, Qwen, Gemma, Nemotron, and GLM (liu2024deepseekv3technicalreport; cheng2026dspark; qwen35; lacombe2026gemma4mtp; nvidia_nemotron_3_ultra_2026; glm5technicalreport), as the default (and sometimes only) supported spec-dec drafter within inference frameworks, e.g. vLLM, SGLang (kwon2023efficient; zheng2024sglang; schmittulms2025speculators). By bundling such a module together with an LLM release, deployment complexity is reduced as there is no need to manage and serve an entirely separate model.

4 A Taxonomy of (Training-free) Relaxed Speculative Decoding

We now provide the reader with an organised summary of a number of relevant approaches in the literature, that aim to relax speculative decoding in a training-free per-token manner. The aim is for the reader to better understand and compare the motivating narratives and concrete algorithms proposed under a unified framework. Tab.˜2 contains a full summary of narrative categorisations and algorithmic details for the different methods investigated in this paper (methods are fully specified when Tab.˜2 is combined with Algorithm˜2).

Bonus token.

Before introducing the methods we discuss a practical detail that applies to all of them. As mentioned before, when all draft tokens are accepted, an additional bonus token is sampled from pp. In the relaxed case, it is possible to replace pp with a relaxed distribution πbonus\pi^{\text{bonus}} (Algorithms˜1 and 2). This can be underspecified in the relaxed spec-dec literature, where πrej,πres\pi^{\text{rej}},\pi^{\text{res}} are generally the main focus.333For example, for the method CACTUS, hao2026cactus do not specify πbonus\pi^{\text{bonus}} in the paper but set it to pp in the published code. In this work, aligning with paper narratives that generally set pp as the capability standard, and code implementations when they are publicly available, we set πbonus=p\pi^{\text{bonus}}=p, other than for Speculative Contrastive Decoding (spec-cont-dec) (yuan-etal-2024-spec-cont-dec), which is the only method that explicitly aims to improve capability over pp and unambiguously defines πbonus\pi^{\text{bonus}}:

πbonus=pfor all methods other than spec-cont-dec where πbonus=πrej=πres.\pi^{\text{bonus}}=p\quad\text{for all methods other than }\texttt{spec-cont-dec}\text{ where }\quad\pi^{\text{bonus}}=\pi^{\text{rej}}=\pi^{\text{res}}. (5)

CACTUS (CACTUS).

hao2026cactus aim to solve the constrained optimisation problem:

π​[q,p,x]∈arg⁡maxr∈ΔV−1\displaystyle\pi[q,p,x]\in\arg\max_{r\in\Delta^{V-1}} min⁡{r​(x)q​(x),1}s.t.\displaystyle\min\left\{\tfrac{r(x)}{q(x)},1\right\}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad DKL​(r∥p)≤α.\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}(r\,\|\,p)\leq\alpha. (6)

i.e. given a draft token xx, maximise the acceptance probability whilst constraining the reverse KL-divergence of the relaxed target from pp. CACTUS optimises given the actual draft token xx, whilst later approaches focus on objectives in expectation over token distributions. A Taylor expansion of the KL-divergence produces the approximate optimum,

πrej​[q,p,x]​(v)=πres={γxif ​v=x,1−γx1−p​(x)​p​(v)otherwise, where ​γx=min⁡{p​(x)+2​α​p​(x)​(1−p​(x)),1},\pi^{\text{rej}}[q,p,x](v)=\pi^{\text{res}}=\begin{cases}\gamma_{x}&\text{if }v=x,\\ \tfrac{1-\gamma_{x}}{1-p(x)}p(v)&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\quad\text{ where }\gamma_{x}=\min\left\{p(x)+\sqrt{2\alpha p(x)(1-p(x))},1\right\}, (7)

where π​(x)\pi(x) is boosted as a function of p​(x)p(x) and other vocab probabilities are uniformly reduced. Notably, if xx is outside of the verifier distribution’s support (e.g. top-pp/kk mask) then it is always rejected regardless of α\alpha, which is a strict behaviour not shared with many of the other approaches. The method thus interpolates between strict spec-dec and verifier-support-based rejection. The narrative is to explicitly control/restrict deviation from pp, whilst maximising speed-up beyond strict spec-dec to provide a good capability-speed trade-off. The main results in hao2026cactus suggest close-to-lossless speed-up can be achieved via relaxation. Additionally, some results suggest capability improves, and the authors speculate in the appendix of (hao2026cactus) that this may be due to an ensembling effect.

Mentored Decoding (mentored-dec).

Tran-Thien_2023 aims to solve a very similar problem as CACTUS,

π​[q,p]∈arg⁡maxr∈ΔV−1\displaystyle\pi[q,p]\in\arg\max_{r\in\Delta^{V-1}} 𝔼x∼q​[min⁡{r​(x)q​(x),1}]s.t.\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\left[\min\left\{\tfrac{r(x)}{q(x)},1\right\}\right]\quad\text{s.t.}\quad DKL​(p∥r)≤α.\displaystyle D_{\text{KL}}(p\,\|\,r)\leq\alpha. (8)

This differs from CACTUS by optimising expected-over-qq rather than draft-token-conditioned acceptance, and by constraining the forward rather than reverse KL from pp. We adopt the version where β=1\beta=1 used in narasimhan2025faster (see their Appendix F.6), as it is simpler and shown to perform very similarly to the full version.444We note that the full version is also more unwieldy to implement as it requires a per-token optimisation over parameters during inference. This gives,

πrej​[p,α]​(v)=min⁡{p​(v)1−α,1},πres​(v)=p​(v),\pi^{\text{rej}}[p,\alpha](v)=\min\left\{\tfrac{p(v)}{1-\alpha},1\right\},\quad\pi^{\text{res}}(v)=p(v), (9)

which boosts πrej​(x)\pi^{\text{rej}}(x) and thus acceptance similarly to CACTUS (compare Eq.˜9 with γx\gamma_{x} from Eq.˜8) and then uses pp for residual sampling after rejection rather than a relaxed target. The narrative in Tran-Thien_2023 is also very explicitly about controlling deviation from pp and maximising acceptance. Thus, mentored-dec is an algorithm that very much belongs in the same bucket as CACTUS both in terms of story and practical implementation.

(Reducible) Fuzzy Speculative Decoding (r-fuzzy).

holsman-etal-2025-fuzzy propose to relax the rejection requirement using a divergence criterion between qq and pp to accept draft tokens if the drafter distribution qq is similar to the verifier pp:

πrej​[q,p]​(v)=πres={q​(v),if Div​(p,q)<α,p​(v),otherwise.\pi^{\text{rej}}[q,p](v)=\pi^{\text{res}}=\begin{cases}q(v),&\text{if }\text{Div}(p,q)<\alpha,\\ p(v),&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases} (10)

We use Jensen-Shannon Divergence as recommended in holsman-etal-2025-fuzzy.555Jensen-Shannon Divergence avoids divide-by-zero issues that may occur with KL-Divergence and non-overlapping support from top-pp/kk. r-fuzzy interpolates between strict spec-dec and always accepting draft tokens from qq. The story is that the drafter can be relied upon to unlock further speed-up if its distribution is similar to the verifier, with a strong focus on enabling a trade-off between capability and speed, which strict spec-dec cannot provide. Note that plugging the above Eq.˜10 into Algorithm˜2 is the “reducible” version of the algorithm, whilst holsman-etal-2025-fuzzy mainly discuss a simpler fuzzy version that directly chooses between qq and pp using the divergence criterion. We only investigate the reducible variant (r-fuzzy) as it has provably better draft acceptance whilst sampling from the same relaxed target distribution (see Appendix˜C for a proof).

Implementation cost.

We find the implementation overhead of relaxed approaches to be roughly the same as strict spec-dec, incurring a slight memory capacity cost increase. 5 Experimental Evaluation

We now present a comparative evaluation of the relaxed speculative decoding methods just discussed. The aim of our experiments is to fairly compare the approaches, to test the validity of the various narratives discussed in Secs.˜4 and 2, and to provide the reader with empirical Findings that may be of use for their own work. We aim to make up for a few shortcomings found in the evaluations of the existing literature. As many of the approaches in Sec.˜4 have been published recently and contemporaneously, they often only compare against a subset of the other methods. As previously mentioned in Sec.˜2.1, existing works generally present narrow evaluations in terms of NdraftN_{\text{draft}} and real-world drafter cost CdrafterC_{\text{drafter}}, and they often neglect the impact of overall generation length on speed-up. Experiments are often performed on out-of-date benchmarks such as CNN/Daily Mail (see-etal-2017-cnndm) and XSum (narayan-etal-2018-xsum) that may not accurately reflect real-world applications. Finally, the impact of the language modelling ability of the drafter is rarely explored, and the methods in Sec.˜4 have yet to be evaluated with the increasingly popular and relevant approach of a bundled dedicated multi-token-prediction (MTP) drafter module.

To this end we measure average accepted length l¯accept\bar{l}_{\text{accept}} over a range of draft lengths Ndraft∈{3,5,10,20}N_{\text{draft}}\in\{3,5,10,20\} and relaxation values α\alpha and then use Eq.˜4, which takes into account generation length, with different potential drafter/verifier relative time cost crel∈{0.05,0.2,0.5}c_{\text{rel}}\in\{0.05,0.2,0.5\} to estimate speed-up over a broad range of settings. We consider 3 drafter+verifier pairs, {Qwen3.5 MTP+27B, Qwen3 0.6B+32B, Qwen3 8B+32B}, that respectively represent a dedicated single-layer MTP drafter module, a weak language model drafter and a strong language model drafter. For our main results we consider AIME 2024 (AIME 24), a challenging maths olympiad dataset that is widely used in model evaluations (aime24). We also evaluate on GPQA Diamond (GPQA) and LiveCodeBench Lite v6 (LCB) (rein2024gpqa; jain2025livecodebench). {AIME24, GPQA, LCB} are modern challenging benchmarks that test a model’s reasoning ability, for which we use thinking mode. To reduce measurement noise from stochastic sampling, we repeat dataset score measurements for each evaluation point {drafter, verifier, NdraftN_{\text{draft}}, method, α\alpha} so that the order of 10310^{3} queries are made to the model. We use standard Qwen stochastic sampling parameters (top-pp/kk) as softmax support truncation is now standard practice for LLM generation (noarov2026toppkused). To test another model family we also evaluate Llama 3.2 1B+3.1 70B (llama3herd) on GPQA. See Appendix˜D for full experimental details (e.g. α\alpha values, evaluation harness etc.).

5.1 Empirical Results and Findings

Fig.˜4 shows our main results, a grid of capability-speed trade-offs over methods and drafter+verifier pairs on AIME24, with highlighted findings. There is variance in capability evaluation due to random sampling of responses, which can be seen in the differences between lossless configurations – standard error lines indicate measurement noise. The reader should be aware when interpreting the results that lightweight drafters will incur lower crelc_{\text{rel}} in reality, even if all drafters are evaluated over the same crelc_{\text{rel}} grid. Full results for AIME24, GPQA, LCB and Llama3 are in Appendix˜E. We will leave discussion of spec-cont-dec to the end as it is distinct from the other approaches.

The similarity of CACTUS and mentored-dec.

As discussed in Sec.˜4, mentored-dec (Tran-Thien_2023) and the more recently published CACTUS (hao2026cactus) are in fact quite similar algorithmically. The left of Fig.˜3 shows that the probability boost given to draft token xx is very similar. We observe generally that both methods behave similarly empirically, shown in the right of Fig.˜3, and so we omit mentored-dec from the main body results for brevity.

The impact of draft length.

Fig.˜4 shows that varying draft length has a considerable impact on achievable speed-up, having comparable or greater impact than relaxation, whilst not requiring any additional capability evaluation. It also demonstrates how the best draft length changes with crelc_{\text{rel}}, demonstrating the need to evaluate over {NdraftN_{\text{draft}},crelc_{\text{rel}}} i.e. showing relaxation gives a very large speed-up over strict spec-dec at Ndraft=20N_{\text{draft}}=20 can be misleading if, for a practitioner’s crelc_{\text{rel}}, Ndraft=5N_{\text{draft}}=5 gives higher overall speed-up and much smaller improvements over strict spec-dec.

Intuitively, a lower optimal NdraftN_{\text{draft}} may impose a lower ceiling on achievable improvement from relaxation. Tab.˜3 shows l¯accept\bar{l}_{\text{accept}} for different NdraftN_{\text{draft}}, where there is clearly much more headroom for improvement at longer draft lengths.

The impact of drafter capability on relaxation.

As previously noted, the community is increasingly moving towards dedicated lightweight MTP drafter modules that are specialised for drafting rather than general language modelling. Moreover, many open serving frameworks only support MTP for spec-dec if it is bundled with the model, i.e. the user can be forced to use MTP rather than freely choose a drafter (see Appendix˜H for a discussion). We find that such MTP modules are naturally unsuited to relaxed spec-dec, precisely due to the fact that they are weak general language models, and so can’t be relied on for relaxation, even if they are decent drafters under strict rejection. Fig.˜4 (C) shows that for Qwen3.5 MTP+27B, only CACTUS/mentored-dec, which tightly control deviation from pp rather than relying on qq, are able to produce a useful capability-speed trade-off. Methods that explicitly rely on qq (e.g. spec-casc-opt, see Tab.˜2) fail with capability drop-offs without speed-ups (discussed later). Using a small general language model (Qwen3 0.6B+32B) gives more useful trade-offs than MTP across evaluated methods, but still shows consistent clear capability degradation, a far cry from the near-lossless capability demonstrated in many relaxed spec-dec papers.

Fig.˜4 (D) actually shows slowdowns vs strict spec-dec in many cases, even though relaxation should increase l¯accept\bar{l}_{\text{accept}} and token throughput. This is because relaxing into a drafter that is a weak language model may cause the model to take longer to reach a “thinking” conclusion, and can even induce generation pathologies (e.g. rambling, repetition loops) that lead to longer generations and overall response times. This reduces speed-up compared to what token throughput or l¯accept\bar{l}_{\text{accept}} would indicate, highlighting the need to consider generation length (Eq.˜4). This is shown in Fig.˜5.999Interestingly, α=0\alpha=0 is the theoretical minimum suggested by (narasimhan2025faster) for spec-casc-opt, as it represents having no “cost” of deferring to the verifier. However, in our experiments, 0 is clearly too high when the drafter is unreliable.

Fig.˜4 (E) actually shows, on the other hand, that the strong drafter (Qwen3 8B+32B) is able to provide much better (sometimes lossless up to measurement noise) trade-offs with relaxation, aligning much closer with results presented in the literature. This is intuitive as a drafter that is a stronger standalone model matches the narrative that qq can be relied on more than strict spec-dec allows, and methods with this story (Tab.˜2) in particular see improvements compared to results with drafters that are weak language models. Moreover, a stronger drafter also intuitively unlocks greater acceleration via longer NdraftN_{\text{draft}}, as the drafter is able to generate longer spans of acceptable text (Tab.˜3). We note the reader should keep in mind that a stronger drafter will incur considerably higher crelc_{\text{rel}} in reality than a lightweight drafter, so realisable speed-up will be reduced in comparison (and Fig.˜4 should be interpreted with this in mind).

This highlights a conflict of narratives where many relaxed spec-dec approaches predicate their methods on the idea that the drafter is a decent language model which can afford relaxation, whilst the community shift towards dedicated MTP drafters leans into the guarantees of strict spec-dec as a crux to disregard general language modelling and focus on faster drafting, whilst only needing to maintain short-range l¯accept\bar{l}_{\text{accept}} under strict rejection. Figs.˜4 and 3 show that the MTP drafter even has higher strict speed-up than Qwen3 0.6B, but worse relaxed trade-offs. As such, although existing methods for relaxed spec-dec are not strictly invalid, they are unsuited for many mainstream drafters.

spec-cont-dec can improve capability at the cost of speed-up.

The narrative of this approach, and thus its empirical behaviour, is different to other relaxed spec-dec methods. Fig.˜4 shows that spec-cont-dec is able to trade-off speed-up for better capability. It meaningfully improves on AIME24 when the drafter is weak, matching the original intuition that pushing the model distribution away from an amateur can improve capability (li-etal-2023-contrastive). This also intuitively explains why speed-up is reduced, as Eq.˜14 pushes π\pi away from qq increasing TV⁡(q,π)\operatorname{TV}(q,\pi) (Eq.˜3). In the case of a strong drafter, we note a degradation in capability, which also aligns with the same intuition, as we wouldn’t expect a non-amateur drafter to produce a useful contrastive signal. We note that with a weak drafter, capability improvement does not happen in every case (e.g. Qwen 3.5 MTP+27B GPQA in Fig.˜8), although in our experiments we do not observe capability degradation beyond measurement noise. This is in line with previous results (obrien2024contrastive), that also demonstrate non-universal improvements. Other relaxation approaches that may have been suggested to improve capability (Tab.˜2) do not demonstrate this in our experiments.

The generalisation of relaxation parameter α\alpha over tasks.

There are no expectations or guarantees that relaxation parameter α\alpha will generalise between tasks, and we find experimentally that the calibration of α\alpha can be quite different between tasks. Fig.˜6 shows that the same values of α\alpha (see Sec.˜D.2 for values) induce markedly different capability-speed trade-offs between AIME24 and GPQA, with the former’s accuracy being much more sensitive to α\alpha than the latter’s.

6 Concluding remarks

In this work, we performed a practical investigation of recent approaches to relaxed speculative decoding. Such methods deviate from the strict guarantee of matching the verifier distribution in pursuit of additional acceleration, controlled capability-speed trade-offs, or potential capability gains. Our investigation suggests that the achievable results are highly setting-dependent. Draft length and relative drafter cost can affect speed-up as much, if not more than, relaxation; weak or dedicated MTP drafters are not reliable language models for relaxed rejection; stronger drafters can potentially give approximately lossless relaxed speed-up; and relaxation parameters do not reliably transfer across tasks. Relaxation can be useful, especially with stronger drafters or methods that tightly control deviation from the verifier, but it should be treated by practitioners as a deployment-specific optimisation potentially requiring further capability evaluation and tuning, rather than a drop-in replacement for strict standard speculative decoding.

Sampling parameters.

For all reported Qwen settings, we use the sampling parameters recommended in the Qwen3 tech report (qwen3) for thinking mode, temperature=0.6, top-pp=0.95 and top-kk=20, omitting additional logit-penalty parameters for simplicity. The standard thinking budget is 32,768 tokens and the maximum generation length is 36,864 tokens. The Qwen3.5-27B native-MTP setting uses a 65,536-token thinking budget and 69,632-token maximum generation length on AIME24 and LCB.

The generation protocol enforces the Qwen thinking budget operationally. Generation proceeds up to the thinking budget. If no </think> token sequence appears, the reasoning block is closed with a fixed early-stop message and </think>, after which answer generation continues within the remaining token budget.

For the Llama GPQA run, we use the Hugging Face generation-config sampling parameters, temperature=0.6 and top-pp=0.9, with no top-kk filtering. Llama does not use a thinking mode; the maximum generation length is 4096 tokens.

Appendix G vLLM speculative decoding

As shown in Tab.˜1, on a single-GPU NVIDIA GH200 Grace Hopper Superchip with vLLM 0.20.1 and PyTorch 2.11.0+cu130, a small drafter is much cheaper when run as an ordinary autoregressive model than when used inside stock vLLM spec-dec. This gap is an implementation effect rather than a property of the drafter alone. In autoregressive decoding, vLLM can compile and capture the repeated decode loop, largely removing CPU-side scheduling and kernel-launch overheads. In separate-draft-model spec-dec, each round must instead coordinate drafting, verification, acceptance, resampling and cache updates, and this work is not collapsed into the same low-overhead loop. Our profiling indicates that the drafter path is therefore dominated by host-side coordination and many small GPU launches, making its effective cost much closer to the target-model token cost than its standalone autoregressive cost. We consequently treat stock vLLM wall-clock speed for separate autoregressive drafters as a diagnostic of current serving overhead, not as an inherent limit of spec-dec. This motivates our use of Eqs.˜2 and 4 to explore potential speed-ups at different drafter costs.

Appendix H MTP Support

We note that current production inference stacks do not always expose spec-dec with a separate autoregressive drafter once a model architecture has native MTP support. That is to say, frameworks sometimes force users to use a bundled MTP drafter if it exists. In vLLM, models recognised as native-MTP-capable are redirected through an MTP-specific configuration path: a draft checkpoint whose architecture matches this family is rewritten to an MTP model class, and the speculative method is subsequently inferred as MTP rather than as a separate autoregressive draft model.161616vLLM lists native-MTP model types in MTPModelTypes: https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/blob/b7f9b6ab271faa621f4cc438fd5ea7ecaf72db8e/vllm/config/speculative.py#L34-L53. Its speculative config override rewrites matching draft configs into MTP architectures, e.g. https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/blob/b7f9b6ab271faa621f4cc438fd5ea7ecaf72db8e/vllm/config/speculative.py#L460-L468, and later dispatches such draft configs to method = "mtp": https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/blob/b7f9b6ab271faa621f4cc438fd5ea7ecaf72db8e/vllm/config/speculative.py#L713-L716. SGLang implements a similar convention for its draft worker: the user-specified draft checkpoint is loaded in a mode that marks it as a drafter, and supported native-MTP architectures are then rewritten to the corresponding MTP implementation instead of retaining the full checkpoint as an independent autoregressive model.171717SGLang’s draft worker selects speculative_draft_model_path while passing is_draft_model=True: https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/blob/50815d54a7b6502342aa037cf462cb1677190a82/python/sglang/srt/managers/tp_worker.py#L329-L341. Its model config then rewrites supported draft architectures to MTP variants, e.g. https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/blob/50815d54a7b6502342aa037cf462cb1677190a82/python/sglang/srt/configs/model_config.py#L473-L488, whose implementation consumes target-side speculative hidden states rather than operating as an ordinary independent LM: https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/blob/50815d54a7b6502342aa037cf462cb1677190a82/python/sglang/srt/models/qwen3_5_mtp.py#L170-L181. Consequently, for such model families, supplying a smaller checkpoint through the standard draft-model interface does not provide a supported route to ordinary two-model spec-dec: the request is either diverted into the MTP implementation, or fails with load- or run-time errors when the resulting assumptions about target-side hidden states, weight names, dimensions, and cache structure are violated.
