Abstract

Recent vision-language models (VLMs) exhibit remarkable generalization and reasoning abilities, yet 3D understanding in these models is limited by data scale, training diversity, and reasoning capacity. Instead of naively extending these models into 3D, we take a different approach: we enable powerful 2D VLMs to operate reliably in 3D by introducing 3D grounding and iterative feedback loops with two novel concepts: Canonical Coordinate Framing (CCF) and Task-Adaptive Feedback (TAF). CCF serves as a unified visual representation that anchors both inputs and outputs to a shared Euclidean coordinate system, solving common challenges in 3D grounding such as axis ambiguity, inconsistent metric scale, and floating references. 1. Introduction

The pursuit of general-purpose 3D understanding and manipulation systems has traditionally relied on specialized

1

models, each tailored for a specific geometric task [26, 33, 55]. Whether converting scanned objects into simulationready models, segmenting parts, or composing scenes from language, these systems depend on extensive 3D annotations, domain expertise, and task-specific training [17, 24, 42, 43]. This specialization fragments the 3D ecosystem and limits scalability to diverse real-world 3D tasks in robotics, graphics, and vision.

Meanwhile, vision-language models (VLMs) trained on massive 2D image-text corpora have demonstrated remarkable generalization, compositional reasoning, and zero-shot learning abilities [11, 14, 47]. Yet, despite their semantic power, these models operate within the 2D image manifold and lack explicit grounding in metric 3D space [51]. Emerging 3D VLMs attempt to bridge this gap, but their generalization remains constrained by limited data scale and diversity [25, 56]. As a result, neither approach provides sufficient performance; directly prompting 2D VLMs for 3D quantities leads to unstable and inconsistent predictions [51], while 3D VLMs lack a diversity of capabilities [56].

We address this challenge by introducing a task-adaptive and grounded 3D programming system built on two novel VLM communication mechanisms: Canonical Coordinate Framing (CCF) for 3D grounding and Task-Adaptive Feedback (TAF). CCF anchors both inputs and outputs to a shared Euclidean coordinate system by rendering multiview orthographic projections with explicit, visible axes, metric scale marks, and a fixed global origin. These visual cues eliminate what we call coordinate-frame ambiguity and allow 2D VLMs to perform spatial reasoning within a consistent geometric frame. While spatial grounding is necessary, it is often insufficient for complex tasks. To address this, we propose TAF, which provides an iterative execution-based feedback loop that serves as a versatile reasoning scratch-space for task-specific information, from physical material descriptions to layout reasoning. Unlike approaches that attempt to merely extend VLMs into 3D with limited data, the combination of CCF and TAF empowers existing 2D models to reason metrically by grounding 3D understanding visually, without retraining or architectural modification. This design transforms pretrained 2D VLMs into geometry-aware systems capable of interpreting, editing, and generating 3D content.

Building on this foundation, we introduce 3D-Prog , a 3D reasoning and generation framework that utilizes our task-adaptive and grounded 3D representation. 3D-Prog takes 3D assets such as point clouds, meshes, or Universal Scene Description (USD) scenes along with a naturallanguage instruction, carries out task-specific analysis, and generates a structured 3D manipulation program. Each line in the program invokes modules for geometric analysis, 3D manipulation, or visual feedback in canonical coordinates. The generated programs consume and produce intermedi-

ate results, forming self-contained reasoning chains that are interpretable and executable. The outputs are written in the USD format, providing a modular, hierarchical, and interpretable representation that is naturally compatible with both 2D VLMs and 3D editing workflows.

Without any retraining or fine-tuning, 3D-Prog performs remarkably well on open-vocabulary 3D understanding, manipulation, and synthesis across object-level and scenelevel tasks. It unifies 3D perception, reasoning, and generation in a visually grounded framework, demonstrating that explicit canonical framing is a prerequisite for robust geometry-aware multimodal reasoning.

Our key contributions are summarized as follows:

  • Canonical Coordinate Framing (CCF) : a representation that visually grounds 3D reasoning for 2D VLMs by enforcing a shared Euclidean coordinate system for inputs and outputs, resolving the coordinate-frame ambiguity.

  • Task-Adaptive Feedback (TAF) : a reasoning scratchspace that enables a CCF-guided VLM to break out of purely geometric reasoning and perform generalist, taskadaptive reasoning over 3D assets.

  • 3D-Prog : a 3D understanding, reasoning, and generation framework building on CCF and TAF. We show that 2D VLMs embedded in 3D-Prog achieve consistent, interpretable, and high-quality results across diverse 3D tasks, without task-specific training or fine-tuning.

2. Related Work

2D VLMs and Emergent Spatial Understanding. Visionlanguage models (VLMs) [3, 11, 14, 22, 47] have demonstrated remarkable multimodal reasoning capabilities across a wide range of 2D tasks, from image captioning to openworld recognition and VQA. Despite being trained on 2D image-text pairs, these models exhibit emergent spatial and physical understanding: they can ground language in object relations, infer affordances, and even support world models and embodied reasoning frameworks [6, 7, 27, 34, 40]. However, their spatial reasoning remains implicitly encoded within 2D coordinates. As recent evaluations highlight [8, 21, 34, 51], these models lack explicit 3D consistency and cannot directly produce geometry-grounded outputs, limiting their use in 3D reasoning and manipulation.

3D-aware VLMs: Bridging to 3D Understanding. To overcome these limitations, several works have introduced 3D-aware VLMs that explicitly model spatial structure or leverage 3D supervision. Some approaches employ 3D backbones [9, 18–20, 38, 57], e.g., point transformers, while others extend 2D models, e.g., with multi-view consistency [16, 23, 31, 46, 54]. These methods achieve strong grounding in 3D perception and have been applied to both understanding tasks (e.g., 3D captioning, grounding, QA) and generation tasks (e.g., text-to-3D synthesis, scene reconstruction). Despite their promising progress,

Visual Programming and Programmatic Reasoning. Beyond model-centric approaches, visual programming frameworks have emerged as a complementary paradigm for integrating perception, reasoning, and action through modular composition. Neural Module Networks [2] pioneered this direction by decomposing visual question answering into structured, executable programs. Building on this idea, recent works such as VisProg [15], ViperGPT [45], and VADAR [37] demonstrate that complex visual tasks can be represented as modular programs, differing mainly in their approach to program synthesis and API generation. However, this entire line of work remains focused on 2D vision tasks (e.g., detection, VQA, image editing), and thus cannot ingest or operate directly on 3D visual inputs. Efforts towards 3D visual program synthesis have so far been domain-specific, targeting single tasks [32, 36, 53] or specialized 3D asset types [35], which limits their generality. In contrast, 3D-Prog supports diverse 3D assets spanning synthetic and scanned data across varying levels of granularity (object- and scene-level), and enables a wide range of 3D tasks, including generation, editing, and understanding.

3. Method

This section presents 3D-Prog , our approach for converting 2D VLMs into 3D programmers capable of generation, understanding, manipulation, and reasoning over 3D assets. To enable 2D VLMs to interact with 3D assets, we first pro-

To support an open-ended range of 3D tasks, we further propose TAF , which allows the model to request and utilize dynamic visual feedback tailored to the instruction, explained in Sec. 3.2. Finally, Sec. 3.3 presents the 3D-Prog framework, which integrates these components into a unified system capable of performing diverse and complex 3D programming tasks.

3.1. Canonical Coordinate Framing

CCF is a 3D grounding representation strategy that visually anchors both 3D inputs and outputs within a shared global Euclidean coordinate system, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Specifically, we render a single visualization with six orthographic projections of the 3D asset, along with a supplementary perspective view for spatial context. The projections share a) visible and labeled X , Y , and Z axes, b) metric scale cues via axis markings, and c) a fixed reference origin anchored to the asset’s bounding box across all views.

By representing 3D geometry through consistent multiview visual-linguistic cues, CCF removes gauge ambiguities and establishes a stable and grounded spatial reference for reasoning. CCF shares its principles with various geometric methods such as DUSt3R [50], MASt3R [29], and VGGT [49] to achieve multi-view consistency in a shared canonical space, enforcing coherence across viewpoints. Within this canonical frame, the VLM is prompted to express predicted 3D coordinates and spatial relationships directly.

3.2. Task-Adaptive Feedback

A key capability of a generalist 3D programmer is the ability to request and obtain task-relevant feedback. While CCF enables seamless interaction between 3D assets and VLMs, the generated asset may still fall short of the task requirements.

To bridge this gap, we propose TAF , a mechanism that establishes a task-dependent feedback loop between the VLM and its generated content. Concretely, we link the VLM to a Python execution environment, enabling it to produce TAF as visual feedback. In each iteration, the VLM generates USD manipulation code and task-specific analysis code that programmatically inspects the current 3D asset, producing diagnostic visualizations (e.g., height distributions, density heatmaps, segmentation overlays). Building upon CCF and TAF, we introduce 3D-Prog, a generalist 3D programming framework that leverages the broad semantic and reasoning capabilities of the 2D VLM to operate effectively on 3D assets. 3D-Prog transforms VLMs into 3D programmers capable of carrying out a wide range of tasks without requiring task-specific supervision or adaptation. Our framework is designed around three core principles: (1) CCF expresses 3D assets in a canonical frame, providing grounding cues that enable VLMs trained on 2D data to infer spatial relationships; (2) TAF equips the system with an iterative feedback mechanism that closes the loop between generation and evaluation; and (3) the USD format, with its hierarchical and compositional nature, aligns scene representation with VLM reasoning patterns, enabling complex 3D transformations to be decomposed into interpretable programmatic steps.

Following these principles, the architecture comprises three primary modules:

2D VLM for perception : CCF-rendered views of the 3D assets serve as the perceptual input to the VLM. Conditioned on these renders and the task description, the VLM produces a) semantic and geometric interpretations of the scene and b) executable code that governs the manipulation of the 3D asset. The generated code is executed inside a

4

Python environment, which provides the essential capabilities of TAF and USD manipulation, enabling the VLM to construct, edit, or transform the underlying USD scene via OpenUSD in Python.

Judge : To provide critical feedback, a separate Judge VLM evaluates the proposed solution. The generated USD asset is rendered and overlaid with the reference model’s CCF representation (Sec. 3.1) to provide iterative visual feedback to the VLM. The judge evaluates the solution based on the user task prompt, comparative visual feedback, and TAF-produced visual analysis. The evaluation yields a structured response comprising a binary completion status, a confidence score, and a diagnostic report (a feedback comment alongside lists of detected errors and prescriptive suggestions). The judge response is appended to the Python output to guide the VLM in subsequent iterations.

Iterative Refinement through TAF : The judge feedback and TAF-produced diagnostic visualizations are fed back to the VLM, which generates an updated USD asset in the next iteration. This TAF-driven loop allows the VLM to correct trivial mistakes (such as faulty USD code, incorrect axes, or wrong rotations), improve geometric fidelity based on visual evidence from its own analysis, and progressively refine its output from coarse to fine-grained solutions. The process continues until the task is successfully completed or a predefined maximum number of iterations is reached.

The range of tasks spans both synthetic and real-world 3D assets at object and scene scales, demonstrating the generalization of 3D-Prog across asset types.

4. Experiments

We evaluate 3D-Prog through task benchmarks, ablation studies, experiments across diverse VLM families, and spatial robustness analysis. We demonstrate that 2D VLMs, when grounded via CCF and paired with TAF, can perform diverse 3D programming tasks effectively.

Datasets. We evaluate 3D-Prog on diverse 3D programming tasks spanning generation, understanding, and reasoning (Tab. S1), covering both object- and scene-level settings with synthetic and real-world data. For Scan2Sim and articulation prediction, we use real-world scans and annotations from the Articulate3D dataset [17] at the object and scene levels, respectively. Semantic and instance part segmentation, object editing, and object completion use synthetic assets from Objaverse-XL [13] and Pixar’s OpenUSD asset library [44], which provide ground-truth part segmentations and semantic labels. For object editing, we curate assets to maximize geometric and visual complexity; for object completion, inputs are generated by removing pre-segmented object parts. For object detection, we combine synthetic data [44] with high-quality laser scans from ScanNet++ [52], which includes human-annotated segmentations. Finally, compositional reasoning is evaluated using five natural-language queries describing complex multiobject indoor environments. The full system and judge prompts are provided in the supplementary material.

Metrics. We evaluate 3D-Prog using task-specific metrics as well as human judgment to capture both geometric fidelity and semantic plausibility. 4.5. Discussion of Experiments

The analysis of 3D-Prog reveals that CCF is essential for tasks fundamentally rooted in geometric reasoning, e.g., object completion or compositional generation. In these settings, resolving gauge ambiguity and providing spatial grounding enable 3D-Prog to converge to a solution consistently over multiple iterations. As expected, for tasks that primarily require appearance changes without geometric adaptations (e.g., simple object editing), the baseline model can achieve a reasonable success score by mapping the task to the 2D domain. Nevertheless, even in these scenarios, our approach outperforms the baseline, showcasing that additional feedback is consistently beneficial.

In a detailed analysis, we observe that visual feedback produced by the TAF module shows a strong degree of specialization tailored to the specific user query and task category. TAF demonstrates the ability to construct specialized visual feedback, generate complex NumPy code, and employ classical ML algorithms such as clustering for segmentation tasks. Furthermore, TAF exhibits an inherently evolutionary nature: across successive iterations, its outputs become increasingly fine-grained by leveraging insights from preceding passes. This evolution from broad analysis to focused refinement is exemplified in the table detection task (Fig. 5). The first iteration generates general cues based on universal rules, while in subsequent iterations, the model shifts to focused asset-specific region analysis. Additional qualitative examples of TAF and a comprehensive failure analysis for both 3D-Prog and the baseline are provided in

the Supplementary Material (Sec. D, E).

Across evaluation tasks, 3D-Prog converges in 7–20 iterations (mean: 11), with each iteration taking approximately 22s on average. Total runtime ranges from around 2 minutes for articulation and QA tasks to 8 minutes for segmentation. VLM inference dominates compute, followed by code execution and rendering. A detailed per-task breakdown is provided in the Supplementary Material.

5. Conclusion

In this work, we presented 3D-Prog, a generalizable approach that enables 2D VLMs to perform 3D tasks, including understanding, reasoning, editing, composition, and generation. 3D-Prog achieves this through CCF, which enables grounding and 3D perception in 2D VLMs, and TAF, which encompasses a flexible scratch-space for taskspecific feedback. Experiments further showcased that 3DProg is a powerful generalist framework that brings the capabilities of current 2D VLMs to free-form 3D programming, unlocking the potential of VLMs for 3D tasks. We demonstrated the versatility and generalizability of 3D-Prog across a diverse set of tasks on both real and synthetic assets, where it consistently and reliably produces highquality 3D outputs and achieves precise 3D understanding.

Limitations. Although powerful, 3D-Prog is not without limitations. First, by design, the performance of 3D-Prog depends on the capabilities of the underlying VLMs, which still struggle with very complex reasoning tasks or finegrained details. Second, 3D-Prog relies on a judge module that evaluates convergence rather than correctness. While 100% motion-type accuracy on articulation suggests the iterative loop is self-correcting, the judge may occasionally accept suboptimal results. We mitigate this through conservative iteration limits and visual comparison feedback. Lastly, 3D-Prog’s ability to process 3D assets relies on the CCF, which is limited in scene size and detail by the VLM context. However, with the fast progress in open and closed VLMs, our method has a clear path towards scaling beyond its current performance.

D.2. Iterative Refinement Case Study: Semantic Segmentation

We perform a case study investigating the TAF visualizations across different 3D-Prog iterations. For the case study, we consider a semantic segmentation task on a synthetic tea kettle instance provided as a point cloud, as shown in Fig. S1.

Iteration 1: Initial Analysis and Hypothesis Formula-

tion. The first visual program checked the asset’s up-axis and traversed the stage hierarchy, establishing that the asset contains a single mesh. Subsequently, the VLM calculated the bounding box and formulated an initial hypothesis that the semantic parts of the kettle are Main Body , Lid , Spout , Handle , and Kettle Base . The generated TAF was a 6-panel plot showing front, right, and top views, two 3D views, and a height distribution histogram.

The generated multi-view point-cloud analysis visualization was utilized to confirm the initial hypothesis regarding the presence of the five target components and to establish the basis for segmentation using Z (height) and R (radial distance from the XY centroid) as primary features.

Iteration 2: Segmentation and Validation. The initial segmentation algorithm employed geometric filtering for the Base and Lid (top 20% of Z -range). The middle section was separated into the Body (points within the 70th percentile radial distance) and an outer region.

The outer points were subjected to DBSCAN clustering to distinguish the Spout and Handle based on size and vertical spread. The second TAF, a 6-panel visualization with semantically colored points, was generated for visual inspection.

Iteration 3: Refinement with Enhanced Spatial Heuristics. The visual inspection of the TAF showed undersegmentation on the Lid and Main Body parts, as well as over-segmentation on the Handle . The VLM refined the initial segmentation algorithm with the following adjustments:

  1. Threshold Adjustment: Height thresholds for the Base and Lid were slightly narrowed (12% and 18%, respectively), and the radial threshold was increased to the 75th percentile to ensure the Handle and Spout were fully contained within the outer region.

  2. Refined Clustering: DBSCAN parameters were adjusted to eps=2.5 and min samples=3 for improved density sensitivity.

  3. Geometric Differentiation Heuristics: The critical refinement was the application of post-DBSCAN geometric heuristics to definitively classify each resulting cluster as either the Spout or the Handle . The Handle was identified by its arc shape and vertical dominance, whereas the

Spout was identified by its relatively compact and linear protrusion.

The legend of the analyzed TAF from iteration 2 also reveals a semantic labeling error—the segment labels were distributed incorrectly (e.g., the Handle was called “Lid”, the Spout was labeled as “Handle”). E. Error Analysis

For each of the runs, we manually inspect the breakdown of the error modes of 3D-Prog. This analysis is essential to understand both the strengths of our method and its current limitations. We categorize the primary error modes as follows:

  • Incorrect Program : The most common failure mode is the method producing erroneous code or invalid USD asset formats. This error mode is more common in the baseline system, often resulting in outputs that cannot be rendered. Iterative feedback significantly reduces these issues in 3D-Prog.

  • Insufficient Geometric Fidelity : The final output sometimes lacks fine-grained geometric details. In some cases, 3D-Prog oversimplifies the geometry, behaving “ lazily ” and failing to capture intricate structures. The independent judge helps reduce this issue by enforcing stronger critical feedback.

  • Trivial Mistakes by Hallucination : These occur when there is a mismatch between the model’s analysis and its final output. In a few runs, 3D-Prog correctly reasons about the scene but fails to transfer the same findings into the generated output, mainly due to hallucinations in the VLMs, as illustrated by the example in Sec.
