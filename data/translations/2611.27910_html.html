Decision Columns as an Index: Interpretable Embeddings for Explainable Entity Matching and Search

Aadharsh Kannan Robert Bruckner Phil Bernstein Microsoft Microsoft Microsoft aadkannan@microsoft.com robruc@microsoft.com philbe@microsoft.com

Abstract

Business data systems often ask two questions. Do these two records describe the same real-world entity ( entity matching )? Which records satisfy a request whose decisive properties are implied rather than stated ( retrieval )? As agents begin to act on business records, the answers need reasons a person can check: why two records were matched, and which fact would change the decision. Dense embeddings answer both questions well but opaquely: no coordinate means anything on its own, a missing fact cannot be added without re-encoding the corpus, and no coordinate can be filtered on. We can address this problem using a decision column that stores, on every row, a decision model’s calibrated answer to a naturallanguage question. We study interpretable embeddings , which use a set of such columns as an index: a record’s vector is its answers to an ordered list of questions ( yes , no or not stated ). Every similarity score breaks down exactly into perquestion terms, and a new question adds a coordinate without changing existing ones. On public product benchmarks we find the following. As the candidate generator of a retrievethen-verify entity matcher, a question space is statistically on par with dense retrieval and explains every accepted match exactly. Questions designed from retrieval failures triple mean reciprocal rank without re-encoding any answer. In search, the request rather than the record is the bottleneck: inferring what the right product will look like beats encoding only what the request says, and question-space scores add to a dense retriever, though not yet at the confidence level we fixed in advance. One small neural network maps an existing dense index to the full interpretable vector, so a large corpus can be backfilled from a few thousand answered rows at about 1/200 of the cost. Interpretable embeddings are best used beside dense retrieval, as a second, readable channel that explains matches, filters as attributes and grows when failures reveal missing facts.

1 Introduction

Two jobs recur wherever business records are integrated and searched. Entity matching decides whether two records describe the same real-world thing: one product listed by two shops, or one customer who arrives from a sales system and a support system as rows of different source or virtual tables. Retrieval finds the records that satisfy a request, and the de-

cisive properties of a request are often implied rather than stated. We follow two real examples, one for each job. In the WDC Products matching benchmark [51], three web shops list desktop memory of the same line and speed: “HyperX Fury Black Series 4GB DDR3 1600MHz CL10 DIMM Memory”, “Kingston HyperX FURY schwarz DIMM Kit 8GB, DDR3-1600, CL10” and “RAM HyperX Fury Black DDR3 1600MHz 2 x 4Go”. The first is a single module; the other two are the same kit of two modules, described in different words. A matcher should join the two kit listings and keep the single module apart. In the STaRK-Amazon product-search benchmark [69], one of the requests written by people reads: “I got wading shoes from Frogg Toggs and love them. I’m now looking for gear to go into deeper rivers. Could you please recommend some stuff?” Seventeen of its 22 correct products are Frogg Toggs waders, yet the request never mentions waders, fishing or legwear. In both jobs the why matters as much as the answer. A data steward who merges two records, or an auditor who reviews the merge later, needs to see which facts made the records look like the same entity and which fact would have changed the decision.

These jobs are increasingly carried out by agents that read and act on business records. An agent that merges duplicate customers, answers a question over several tables or shortlists a product must be able to say what it relied on. Agents, and the people who supervise them, therefore need representations that can be queried as ordinary attributes, extended when a gap appears and cited when an action is taken. This paper does not evaluate agents; the requirement motivates the representation we study.

Dense text embeddings [30, 54, 70], vectors produced by a trained neural encoder, are the default representation for both jobs. They are accurate but opaque. No coordinate means anything on its own, so an explanation of why two records are close has to be reconstructed after the fact [2]. A fact the encoder never learned, such as whether a memory product is a multi-module kit, cannot be added without re-encoding the whole corpus with a new model. A coordinate cannot be filtered on, because it is not an attribute, and some business databases cannot index caller-supplied vectors at all.

What has changed is the cost of asking. A decision model answers a question about a record by returning a calibrated probability for each of a fixed set of outcomes rather than by generating text; calibrated means that outcomes given probability 0.8 occur about 80% of the time. Such models are

A maker writes a question, such as “Is this memory product sold as a multi-module kit?”, and every row receives a calibrated answer, stored as an ordinary column that can be filtered and sorted. A system can also create such columns in the background, as an index that users never see, to support matching and retrieval. The answers then form a vector space in which every coordinate is readable.

An interpretable embedding makes that space explicit (§2). A question space is an ordered list of natural-language questions. For each record, a decision model gives probabilities for yes , no and not stated , and the record’s coordinate for a question is 𝑃 ( yes ) − 𝑃 ( no ): +1 for a confident yes , −1 for a confident no and 0 when the record is silent. The similarity of two records is a sum of per-question terms, so the score of the single module and the 8GB kit breaks down exactly into named terms, and the only negative one is “one is a multi-module kit, the other is not (−)”. Under the dot product, removing a term changes the score by exactly that term, so every explanation is also a valid counterfactual. Adding a question adds a coordinate and leaves every existing answer untouched, and any coordinate can be promoted to an ordinary column for filtering.

This work builds on question-answer embeddings. QA-

Emb [7] introduced vectors of a large language model’s (LLM’s) yes/no answers and grew its questions from higherror examples, and CQG-MBQA [59] scaled the idea with contrastive question generation and a distilled encoder. We extend this line in two directions. First, we answer with a decision model that returns calibrated probabilities over explicit outcomes, rather than reading a probability off an LLM’s generated token, and its not stated outcome separates silence from evidence against. Second, we treat a question space as a component of a data system and test it on the two jobs above.

How does this help entity matching? Practical matchers first retrieve a few candidate records for each incoming record and then check each candidate pair with a slower, more accurate verifier . We use the question space as the candidate generator. In our runs, the single module and the 8GB kit agree on every question both answer except whether the product is a kit, so they are retrieved as a candidate pair, and the verifier rejects it. The two kit listings agree on every question both answer, the kit question included, and the verifier accepts them as one product. Because the candidate score breaks down into per-question terms, a reviewer can see what made the listings look alike and that the kit question alone set the single module apart. Every accepted match carries such an account.

How does it help retrieval? The decisive properties of a request are often implied, as in “gear to go into deeper rivers”.

Asking the record questions of the request literally leaves most answers not stated . Instead, a model infers what the right product will look like in question space (here, fishing gear worn as a full-body garment over the legs: waders), and records are scored against that inferred target. This is a form of intent expansion: the target states what the request implies. Question-space scores can also be added to a dense retriever’s scores, and when a request fails because no question names the missing fact, a question that names it can be added to the space without re-encoding anything.

We ask whether a readable representation can do the work of dense embeddings at acceptable accuracy and cost while providing explanation, filtering and growth by construction. We report results on two public product benchmarks, WDC Products [51] for entity matching and STaRK-Amazon [69] for product search, and reserve the internal benchmarks that motivated the question. Our main tests were pre-registered : hypotheses and pass bars were fixed before a held-out test set was scored, once. Table 1 defines this and the other terms and metrics we use.

In brief, a question space matches products as accurately as dense retrieval while explaining every match. Used alone for search it trails dense retrieval, mostly because short requests are hard to encode; but it grows where it fails, improves when a model infers the intended product, adds a consistent but not yet confirmed gain to dense search, and can be filled in cheaply from an existing dense index. Its value is as a second, readable channel beside dense retrieval rather than as a replacement for it.

2 Interpretable embeddings

An interpretable embedding has four parts: questions, answers, coordinates and a score that breaks down by question (Figure 1). A cache key per question and a way to grow the list of questions make a space behave like a set of columns rather than a frozen model.

Each dimension has a content hash ℎ𝑑 over the prompt version, the preface, the answering model, the table, the state template and that dimension’s own definition, and over no other dimension . Every stored answer is fresh , stale or missing with respect to ( ℎ𝑑, record input hash). A space hash over the ordered ℎ𝑑 , the encoding and the weights ties any index to the exact questions behind it.

Encoding a request. A search request must also become a vector in the same space. The literal, or symmetric , encoder uses the request text as the state and asks the same questions about “the product the shopper wants, as the request states or clearly implies”, choosing not stated when the request is silent. A requirement variant rewords each question as a requirement (Appendix C). Section 5.2 replaces literal encoding with targets inferred by an LLM.

Growing a space. A seed space is induced by an LLM ( gpt-5.6-luna ) from groups of sample records. It then grows in rounds, in the spirit of QA-Emb’s error-driven question generation [7]. Candidate questions are written from a census of training requests and an analysis of retrieval failures (in our runs, by an LLM coding assistant); a cheap model answers every candidate on every row; candidates must pass admission rules on coverage, refusals and redundancy; and greedy forward selection by MRR on a training split picks a fixed number of them. A random selection from the same admitted candidates is the control. Seed and round counts are in Appendix C.

3 Data and evaluation

Entity matching: WDC Products. We use the 80%-cornercase variant of WDC Products [51], a benchmark of product offers from many web shops: 11,715 offers of 2,162 products, with a seen and an unseen test set of 1,000 offers of 500 products each. Unseen products do not occur in the training data, and every test offer has exactly one true match. Dense retrieval uses bge-base-en-v1.5 [70], and the keyword baseline is BM25 [55], a classic score based on term frequencies; encoder fields and BM25 settings are in Appendix C.

Product search: STaRK-Amazon. STaRK-Amazon [69] pairs shopper requests with the Amazon products that satisfy them, among 957,192 products. Asking 128 questions about every product was outside our budget, so every method ranks the same fixed pool of 25,919 products: every correct product of every request plus the top keyword and dense candidates for each request (Appendix C). Pool results are therefore not comparable with published full-catalogue results. We test on STaRK’s 281 test requests (81 written by people and 200

synthesised); design and tuning used only splits of STaRK’s training and validation requests, called fit, dev and val.

Pre-registration and evidence. Each growth round, the matching gate, the query-target study and the backfill evaluation were pre-registered : hypotheses, comparisons, pass bars and bootstrap seeds were recorded before the sealed test split was scored, and that split was scored once per study. Intervals come from the bootstrap: the test requests or offers are resampled with replacement and the metric is recomputed, on the same resample for both systems when two are compared. Results are tagged confirmatory, exploratory or descriptive as defined in Table 1; the tags appear in captions and in the claims table (Table 8). Resample counts and seeds are in Appendix J.

4 Explainable entity matching at parity

A question space can replace dense retrieval as the candidate generator of an entity matcher without losing accuracy, and every match it accepts then comes with an exact explanation. This matters wherever a data steward, auditor or agent has to justify a merge: the explanation names the facts on which two records agree and the fact that would change the decision.

Setup. We use a standard retrieve-then-verify matcher on WDC Products (§3). Each test offer retrieves its top five candidates, and a verifier (MD1, asked “Are these the same product?”) scores every candidate pair; pairs above a threshold tuned on validation data are matches. Only the candidate generator changes between systems. Our generator uses a 35-dimension WDC space: the 32 seed and round-1 yes/no questions plus three choice dimensions for storage size, colour and pack count. It uses hard encoding, equal weights and the dot product, and blends in a BM25 title score with a share of 0.4, so that the title appears as one more named term. A pre-registered gate, fixed before the verifier ran, defined parity: on both the seen and the unseen test set, the generator had to stay within 5 points of dense retrieval in candidate recall at 10 and within 2 points of dense in F1 with the same verifier, and every accepted match had to pass the removal check below.

Removal check. An explanation is faithful if it correctly predicts what happens when its terms are removed. We delete the five largest positive and five largest negative terms of a candidate pair, recompute the score and require it to move by the predicted amount within 0.05. Under the dot product with hard encoding, every WDC candidate pair passes (error ≈ 10−17 ); under cosine with evidence encoding, none does (mean error 0.088), because removal also changes the norms.

5.1 Growing the space

Adding questions improves search, and the questions that help most are the ones chosen by measured gain. Because every answer is cached under its own question (§2), a new question asks for one new answer per record and leaves every existing answer untouched. Packed into the call that already scores a record, one more MD1 question costs about 115 billed tokens per record, and answering 64 new questions for every record in the STaRK pool took about 27 minutes (Appendix D). A dense encoder has no such step: a new fact needs a new model and a re-encoded corpus.

The space grew through the two pre-registered rounds of §2, each required to gain at least 5 MRR points on the sealed test. An LLM induced the 32 seed questions from sample records. 5.2 The request is the bottleneck

Used alone, even the 128-question space ranks well below text retrievers, and the reason lies in the request rather than the records. With the literal encoder of §2, it reaches 21.9 MRR, against 52.5 for dense retrieval and 53.7 for BM25 (Appendix B). Yet a diagnosis of the 64-question space on the development requests shows that the records are described well enough: using the correct product’s own vector as the query ranks it in the top 20 for 97.0% of requests (counting ties in its favour). The loss arises in turning a short request into a vector. Asked literally, the record questions leave most of a request’s answers not stated , weigh every question equally and, on judgement-laden questions, often contradict the correct product: in 61% of that space’s development failures, a request answer contradicted the correct product’s record.

We therefore change what is asked about the request. Instead of encoding what the request says, a model emulates the shopper. Three ways of doing so score the same 128-question record vectors; only the target changes (Appendix F). The importance-weighted request keeps the literal requirement vector but weights each question by how much it matters to the shopper, as judged by gpt-6-luna on a scale of essential, helpful or unimportant. The inferred target gives a second model ( gpt-6-astra ) the request, all 128 questions and each question’s share of yes answers in the catalogue, and asks it in one call how the product the shopper will buy would be labelled. Silence becomes the catalogue prior instead of a zero, and the question changes from “what does the request say?” to “what will the answer look like?”. Hypothetical listings , following hypothetical document embeddings (HyDE) [18], have the model write three realistic listings for products the shopper would buy, without seeing our questions; the record encoder then encodes them. Scores are normalised per request and summed with weights chosen on the fit split.

6 Backfill from an existing dense index

Asking every question of every record is the main cost of an interpretable embedding, but most of it can be avoided. Many corpora already have a dense embedding for every record, and one small network can predict the whole interpretable vector from it. The answering model, the teacher , answers a sample of a few thousand records; a multi-layer perceptron (MLP), a small feed-forward neural network, learns as the student to map each record’s dense vector to the teacher’s answers; and the student fills in every other record. A corpus with a dense index can thus gain a readable one at a small fraction of the cost. Unlike distillation into a new text encoder [59], which must still re-read every document, the student starts from a vector that already exists.

Setup. Protocol, metrics, seeds and model selection were fixed in code before any model was trained. Each of five pair sets pairs an existing dense vector with the teacher’s answers for exactly the same record: STaRK products with the 32 seed questions answered by MD1 and with the 64-question space, two controls with a gpt-6-luna teacher or a smaller dense encoder as input, and WDC offers with 32 MD1 questions. Splits keep near-duplicate records together. One MLP predicts the whole vector with a single loss, and the baseline is ridge regression, a regularised linear map (Appendix H). 7 Related work

Question-defined and concept embeddings. Our work builds most directly on QA-Emb [7], which represents a text by a general-purpose LLM’s yes/no answers to a list of questions and applies the vectors mainly to predicting fMRI responses, with smaller retrieval and clustering evaluations. QA-Emb introduced the probability of yes as a coordinate, learned per-question weights, answers averaged over two LLMs, a distilled encoder with one head per question, growing questions from high-error examples “similar to gradient boosting”, and fusion with BM25, which raised MS MARCO MRR from 0.77 to 0.80 (on a 0–1 scale). CQG-MBQA [59] scales the idea: it generates questions contrastively from corpus clusters, trains a frozen encoder with one MLP head per question, and evaluates semantic similarity, retrieval and clustering as the number of questions grows. Concurrent work grounds questions in a biomedical ontology [60] and organises them hierarchically [45], finding shallow questions better under a small budget. LDIR [64] derives interpretable coordinates from relatedness to anchor texts, and Opitz et al. [47] survey interpretable text embeddings and similarity explanations. We

extend this line by answering with a decision model whose output is a calibrated distribution over explicit outcomes rather than an LLM’s token probability, by mixing answering models per question, by measuring appendable growth against a random-question control, and by evaluating question spaces in entity matching, search augmentation and corpus backfill; Appendix G covers topic discovery.

The family also descends from concept bottleneck models [33] and their label-free and LLM-generated variants [39, 46], which predict human-named concepts before a task head; Ludan et al. [39] likewise grow the concept set from errors, for classification. Sparse interpretable word vectors [14, 58] and features learned by sparse autoencoders (SAEs) [10, 13], including SAEs over dense-retriever embeddings [48], discover features first and name them afterwards. Question spaces name the feature first and measure it afterwards.

Models as annotators. Each coordinate is a model annotation. LLMs can match or exceed crowd annotators [19] and judge search relevance [61]; their token probabilities are partially calibrated [22, 29], and verbalised confidences can

9

be better calibrated [62]. monoT5 [44] uses the probability of a “true” token as a relevance score. We use the same kind of signal over many questions per record, taken from a decision model that returns calibrated probabilities for a fixed set of outcomes, so that the size of a coordinate is a usable measure of evidence.

Retrieval, fusion and query expansion. We compare against BM25 [55] and a BGE dense encoder [70], in the family of DPR, E5 and Contriever [25, 30, 65]. Late interaction [31] and learned sparse retrieval [16] trade readability for accuracy in other ways; SPLADE’s coordinates are vocabulary terms, whereas ours are questions that can name properties no single term expresses. We combine retrievers with normalised score interpolation [11, 67] rather than rank fusion [12]. HyDE [18] and Query2doc [66] have an LLM write a hypothetical document for the query, and generative relevance feedback [40] uses LLM text as feedback, in the tradition of relevance feedback [34, 56]. We carry HyDE into question space by encoding hypothetical listings with the row encoder, and we add a further step in which an LLM writes the target vector directly.

Product search and entity matching. STaRK [69] provides semi-structured product-search queries over Amazon products, and field-aware retrievers such as mFAR [37] exploit its structure. WDC Products [51] benchmarks matching product offers across shops, with seen and unseen products and many corner cases; fine-tuned matchers such as Ditto [38] and LLM matchers [50, 52] are the reference systems. Faceted search [63] is the classic interface for attribute filters; promoted question dimensions act as facets defined in natural language. To our knowledge, this is the first evaluation of question spaces on STaRK and WDC Products.

Mapping between representations. Knowledge distillation [24] trains a cheap student on a teacher’s outputs. Model stitching [5, 36], relative representations [43] and unsupervised embedding translation [27] show that one representation can often be mapped into another, and probing [1, 6] and concept activation vectors [32] read human concepts out of hidden states. Vec2text [42] shows that dense text embeddings retain most of their input. Our backfill MLP applies these ideas as a production student that maps an existing dense index to a whole question space, and we follow Belinkov [6] in reading its accuracy as recoverability rather than proof of containment.

Explanations and filtered search. Explainable IR [2], explanation-augmented rankers [15] and explainable recommendation [71] usually add explanations to an opaque scorer. Following Rudin [57], we use a scorer that is interpretable by construction, and following Jacovi and Goldberg [26], we separate faithfulness of the score to its terms from faithfulness of the answers to the record. Approximate nearest-neighbour indexes [28, 41] and filtered variants [20, 49] support vector search under predicates; question answers can also be stored as ordinary attributes, so many filters need no vector index at all.

8 Discussion and conclusion

A second, readable channel. Table 8 summarises the evidence. Interpretable embeddings trade some retrieval accuracy for properties that dense vectors lack: named and checkable coordinates, growth without re-encoding, a per-question choice of answering model, and filtering and explanation by construction. Used alone, they retrieve well below a dense encoder. As the candidate generator of a verified matcher they match its accuracy, and beside it in search they add a consistent gain that is not yet confirmed. Their value is therefore as a second, readable channel attached to every record. It explains each match question by question, can be queried and filtered as ordinary attributes, grows when a failure exposes a missing fact, and can be backfilled cheaply from the dense index once a sample has been answered. For the people and agents who act on business records, a match or a ranking can then be cited in terms of named facts, and a missing fact can be added as a question rather than by retraining an encoder. The correct product’s own vector would rank it in the top 20 for 97% of requests, and changing only the request target gained 12.3 MRR points on the same records, so better request modelling is the most promising route to stronger search.

Other uses. The same answers support uses we report in the appendices. Clustering question vectors yields topics whose over-represented answers name them: on STaRK, read as membership rules, the generated labels have mean precision 0.845, against 0.197 for random rules of matched prevalence, and are computed without reading a document or calling a model (Appendix G).

Practical guidance. Use the space beside dense or lexical retrieval, or to filter, verify and explain, rather than as the only retriever. Design questions from failures and select them by measured gain, preferring questions about positive identity (what the record is ) over judgements drawn from reviews: requests that affirm identity questions are retrieved more reliably, and such questions are recovered better from dense vectors. Encode requests by inferring the target record rather than by literal reading. For a corpus with an existing dense index, label a few thousand records with the teacher, use ridge below about 2,000 records and the MLP above, and route the riskiest records back to the teacher.

Limitations. Used alone, a question space retrieves well below dense retrieval: 21.9 MRR with the literal encoder and 34.2 with the best request targets, against 52.5. The denseaugmentation gain did not meet its pre-registered interval. Both datasets are English product data, and STaRK results use a fixed pool of candidate products rather than the full catalogue; other domains, languages and record types are untested. Each answer is a model’s inference. We rely on the decision model’s calibration and did not measure it against gold labels on these tasks; a judge model’s agreement with MD1 tracks MD1’s confidence (Appendix D), but the judge shares a model family

10

with the question proposer, so this is a sanity check rather than ground truth. All backfill numbers treat the teacher’s answers as truth, and a new domain needs its own teacher sample. Each pre-registered study scored the STaRK test split once, but several studies used it in turn, and later studies were designed after earlier results were known; fit, dev and val were exposed during design and serve as monitors. Costs assume a CPU price and one model’s pricing.

Broader impact. Interpretable coordinates make a representation easier to audit: a reviewer can see what was asked, what was answered and how each answer moved a score. The same property makes it easy to filter records by inferred attributes. When records describe people, the questions are the place to enforce policy, and questions about protected or sensitive attributes should not be asked. Each answer is a model’s inference from the record, not a verified fact, and should be presented as such.

Conclusion. Interpretable embeddings turn a list of questions into a vector space whose every coordinate can be named, checked, grown and filtered, and whose every similarity breaks down exactly into per-question terms. Answered by a decision model with an explicit not stated outcome, such a space matches dense candidate generation in verified entity matching while explaining every match. It grows where search fails without re-encoding a stored answer, it searches better when a model infers what the right product looks like, and its scores add to dense ranking, though not yet at our pre-registered level. One small network trained on a few thousand answered records extends it to the rest of a corpus that already has dense embeddings, at a small fraction of the cost.

B Question spaces as stand-alone retrievers

Question vectors can be put into any standard embeddingretrieval stack: an exact scan, an approximate nearestneighbour index (Appendix D) or a rank-fusion pipeline. This appendix measures how they perform when used that way.

Setup. On the STaRK pool (§3), queries are encoded with the symmetric v1 encoder ( gpt-6-luna ), and pool rows are ranked by the unweighted dot product with the row vectors; both choices were the pre-registered primary scoring, fixed on fit with a dev guard. Bootstrap intervals are paired over queries (10,000 resamples).

Results. Even I128 is 30.7 MRR points below dense retrieval, −30 . 66 [−36 . 50, −24 . 93] in a post hoc paired bootstrap over the same per-query ranks [D], and I64 was −36 . 26 [−41 . 88, −30 . 61] below [C] (Table 9). D.3 Filterable

Each coordinate is an attribute with a stated meaning, so it can be filtered on directly. A question can be promoted into an ordinary database column that can be filtered, sorted and chained, which matters on platforms that cannot index caller-supplied vectors. A ranking can also be computed from stored answers alone with wanted answers (Eq. 4). With MD1 choosing wanted answers for the 32 seed questions at query time, this reached Hit@1 7.8 and MRR 12.9 on the STaRK test set, with no vectors and no index [E]. That is weaker than any text retriever but stronger than the symmetric I32 vector query (7.3). Positive filters are also selective: keeping only rows that answer yes to every question a query strongly affirms leaves a median of 51.5 of 25,919 rows for queries that I64 ranks well (Appendix B).

D.4 Indexable

Question vectors are small and dense, so they also drop into standard vector search. On synthetic resamples of real vectors at 106 × 256, HNSW [41] answered queries in 0.61 ms at the median with recall@10 of 0.949, rising to 0.990 at ef = 256 [D]. Used as the only retriever, however, they rank well below text retrievers (§5.2; Appendix B).

D.5 Exactly explainable

Under cosine, the removal-check error of §4 has mean 0.088 and maximum 0.146. For each WDC test query and its top-1 neighbour under cosine, the median number of terms needed to reach 50%, 80% and 90% of the score is 12, 20 and 23. Most of the mass is shared no answers, 92–96% of absolute contribution; grouped into one “neither is X” item, a single term reaches 80% [E]. On STaRK, every one of 4,643 retrieved hits in the full search flow carried a term-level explanation.

The decomposition is faithful to the answers, which are only as good as the answering model [26]. An LLM judge ( gpt-5.6-luna ) agreed with MD1 on 78.8% [77.1, 80.4] of sampled cells of the WDC seed space [C], and on 93.8% of the 470 cells where both gave a definite answer. D.6 Stable

MD1 returned bit-identical answers for 256 of 256 cells across five replicates [D]. Each of eight WDC questions was paraphrased twice by an LLM, and under a pre-set stability rule all eight were stable: the top answer changed on 0–3.8% of rows, and mean |Δ 𝑃 ( yes )| was 0.004–0.025 [C]. Answers are, however, sensitive to irrelevant context: appending 4k, 16k and 64k characters of unrelated rows moved answers by 0.09, 0.36 and 0.49 on average [D], which is why the state includes only rows about the record itself. Appending 128k characters exceeded the answering model’s context limit.

G Topic discovery with self-describing clusters

A cluster of question vectors is a cluster of answer profiles , so its over-represented answers name it, with no LLM summarisation and without reading any member document. Topic models name clusters by frequent words [9, 21], and LLM pipelines read sampled documents [53]. Our approach follows work that describes clusters with natural-language predicates [68, 72], with predicates that are fixed questions already answered for every row.

Related work. LDA [9], Top2Vec [3] and BERTopic [21] discover topics from words or dense clusters and name them from term statistics; TopicGPT [53] asks an LLM to propose and assign topics. Goal-driven clustering [68] and explaining datasets in words [72] describe clusters with natural-language predicates. We cluster with E-Means [8], an exponentialmoving-average mini-batch 𝑘 -means, and choose label predicates over pre-defined, already answered questions by answer statistics.

Asymmetry and containment. The mapping is asymmetric [C]. The reverse MLP has 1,451,533 parameters; ridge explains 21.4% of the dense vector’s variance. For reference, the dense vector’s top 32 principal components explain 45.9% and its top 64 explain 60.6%, and 32 random projections explain 24.1%; 64 answers carry about as much as 34 principal components. The reverse model was still improving at 300 epochs, so these are lower bounds.
